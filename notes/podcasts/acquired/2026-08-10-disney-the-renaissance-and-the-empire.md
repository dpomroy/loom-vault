# Notes: Disney: The Renaissance and the Empire

## Summary

Disney's deepest strength is its ability to build **structural flywheels** — and its deepest vulnerability is mistaking the flywheel for the business itself.

In 1984, the company made just $2 million from film and TV. Eisner, Wells, and Katzenberg fixed that with disciplined cash management and a simple insight borrowed from Broadway: give your lead character an "I want" song in the third scene, and audiences will follow her anywhere. That formula powered the Renaissance and turned animated films into theme park rides, musicals, and merchandise worth hundreds of millions each.

The ABC acquisition in 1995 looked like a content deal. Hidden inside it was **ESPN** — a cable toll booth that raised carriage rates 20% a year, couldn't be dropped by any operator, and generated 60% of Disney's operating income at its peak. That cash funded Pixar ($7.4B), Marvel ($4B), and Lucasfilm ($4B) — three of the best acquisitions in corporate history. But ESPN also contaminated Disney's identity. Mickey Mouse and SportsCenter have nothing in common, and the tension eventually forced Eisner out.

Cord-cutting ended the ESPN era around 2015. Disney+ was the response, but streaming is structurally hostile to Disney's model. Netflix-style volume erodes the scarcity that makes Disney IP feel premium. Every mediocre Marvel series shrinks the brand a little more.

Parks now generate nearly 60% of operating income. They are the flywheel that remains. But the franchise pipeline feeding them is thinning — every major earner traces back to acquisitions made fifteen to twenty years ago, and no genuinely new franchise has broken through since.

Disney is not dying. Generational myths don't die. But the easy structural tailwinds are gone, and no one has found the next one yet.

## The 1984 Creative Collapse

### "In 1984, Disney generated a quarter billion dollars of profit from its parks and consumer products and a mere $2 million from film and TV." [00:05:07]

The core flywheel was already dead before the raiders even arrived. That ratio — 125:1 parks vs content — shows how badly the creative engine had seized up. A company built on story was surviving purely on real estate.

### "We have no obligation to make art. We have no obligation to make history. We have no obligation to make a statement. But to make money, it is often important to make history, to make art, or to make some significant statement." [00:11:33]

Eisner's Paramount memo is still one of the cleanest articulations of commercial creative strategy ever written. The framing matters: quality as *instrument*, not obligation. That's not cynical — it's just honest about incentives. Most studios never figure this out.

### "Celebrity can open a film, but celebrity can't carry a film." [00:11:33]

Katzenberg's line is the tighter, more quotable version of the same idea Eisner was writing memos about. Worth keeping next to the Eisner quote — they're two sides of the same thesis: story is the product, talent is the marketing.

### "27 of their first 33 movies that they make are profitable, which, in Hollywood, to have a hit rate like that, that's like making 33 venture capital investments and only losing money in six of them." [00:11:33]

That hit rate is the empirical proof that the singles-and-doubles strategy wasn't just philosophy — it actually worked at scale. The VC analogy is apt. Most studios were swinging for home runs and striking out; Eisner was running a returns-first content fund.

## The Broadway Formula and the Renaissance

### "In every great Broadway musical, the third song, the leading lady goes and she sits on something, might be a rock, might be a trash can, and she sings a song to the world telling the audience what she wants." [00:24:30]

Howard Ashman's structural insight — the "I want" song — is the actual creative engine behind the Renaissance, not the executives. Eisner and Katzenberg created conditions; Ashman created the formula. That distinction matters when you're thinking about where value really sits in a creative org.

### "Once upon a time, Disney movies got beat at the box office by rom-coms. The Little Mermaid made less money than When Harry Met Sally, in 1989." [00:37:33]

The Renaissance wasn't an instant explosion — it required home video to unlock the real returns. Box office was the green shoot; VHS was the harvest. A good reminder that distribution format can be as value-creating as the content itself.

### "The Lion King musical has grossed over $11 billion in total revenue in its run, which I think makes it the single highest-grossing entertainment product in history in any medium"

The Lion King musical — not the film, not a franchise — is the
single highest-grossing entertainment product ever made.
Most people would guess a Marvel film or Star Wars.
The answer being a **Broadway show** is a genuinely
counterintuitive fact worth holding onto.
$350M average gross per year, for 30 years, owned outright.

### "You should think each of those is half a billion dollars in Disney's bank account."

Disney was pulling ~50% cash flow margin on VHS sales —
so 32 million Lion King tapes meant roughly $500M in profit,
on top of $250M already made at the box office.
This is the flywheel made literal: one film,
**two massive profit events**, minimal incremental cost.

### "It doesn't make people any less excited to go to the theater 6, 7 years later."

Eisner's insight was that home video didn't cannibalise
theatrical demand — kids lose tapes, parents rebuy them,
and the theatrical re-release cycle still worked.
The demand for IP is **not a fixed pool**.
Exposure breeds more demand. Disney understood this
before almost anyone else did.

## ESPN: The Accidental Cash Machine

### "Buried within ABC was the single best cable asset in the history of mankind."

Broadcast networks like ABC had been degraded by cable competition,
losing ad dollars and relevance — but sitting inside ABC
was ESPN, which had become the dominant cable force
precisely because of that same cable boom.
Eisner was buying a **weakening network**
and getting the strongest sports monopoly ever built.
Classic deal-within-a-deal.

### "All ESPN has to do is threaten to pull the plug on any given cable operator"

ESPN's leverage was simple: sports rights were so
emotionally charged that if a cable operator dropped ESPN,
subscribers would show up furious at the cable company's door.
Americans love their sports above almost everything.
This is **pure pricing power** — not product quality,
not switching costs, just the credible threat of
withdrawing something people consider non-negotiable.

### "Those three guys didn't just save Disney. They made Disney flourish in a way that Walt only could have dreamed of."

Operating profit went from under $300M to nearly $2B
in a single decade — almost 8x — after it had been
actively declining before Eisner, Wells and Katzenberg arrived.
Strong claim, but the numbers back it.
The **dream team framing** also sets up how badly
the collapse of that team (Wells dead, Eisner hospitalised,
Katzenberg gone) damaged what came next.

### "Just call up the cable operators every year or 2 and say, hey, we're going to raise carriage rates 20%. Oh, you don't like that? Okay, we'll pull the plug."

The business model in its purest form.
No product innovation required — just leverage.
This is what a monopoly supplier looks like in practice.

### "Mickey Mouse and SportsCenter do not meet."

Five words that expose the whole tension
of the Cap Cities deal.
Disney bought a business that had nothing
to do with being Disney.

### "It gave Disney a scale to remain independent while other studios were falling on hard times. ESPN also further stabilized the business during the ups and downs of animation."

Iger's own words confirm ESPN wasn't just
a cash cow — it was **existential insurance**.
Without it, Disney likely gets absorbed
in the studio consolidation wave.

### "Walt Disney's model was a nice side business to the real moneymaker."

Ben Thompson's framing is brutal and correct.
The famous Disney flywheel — beloved,
celebrated, taught in business schools —
was structurally smaller than a cable carriage deal.
**Scale beats elegance.**

### "Disney never again would get to be just Disney."

The real cost of the ESPN windfall.
Not money — **clarity**.
Every strategy decision from 1996 onward
had to serve two completely different businesses.

### "ESPN is arguments one, two, and three of why was he a great CEO."

Ben's ranking is right.
The Disney Renaissance gets all the cultural credit,
but ESPN is what made Eisner's numbers
look like a **generational run**.

## Pixar: Built on Compounding Accidents

### "Well, I don't see how things will be any different, but sure, when the dust settles, be in touch."

Jobs to Iger on the phone the day Iger
was named CEO. Classic Steve: dismissive
opener, then leaves the door open.
That throwaway call becomes the pivot
point for the entire Disney acquisition
of Pixar.

### "He gets asked if it's gonna be faster and cheaper than traditional animation, 'cause why else are they doing it? And he says neither."

Lasseter fired same day for answering
honestly. Disney's management in the
early '80s couldn't conceive of value
that wasn't cheaper or faster — exactly
the failure mode that let Pixar eat their
lunch a decade later.

### "This all happened because George believed so deeply in Star Wars and the movies he was making that he didn't want to give up any equity in Lucasfilm as part of his divorce."

Lucas protecting his equity from a
divorce settlement is the butterfly
that creates Pixar. The entire $7.4B
Disney-Pixar outcome traces back to
one man's refusal to dilute his cap table.

### "If it's working in our story reels, when we animate it and put color to it, it's gonna work even better. If it's not working in story reels, the animation won't save it."

Lasseter's process insight is
underrated. They built a low-res 2D
prototype before committing to the
expensive 3D render pipeline — basically
MVP thinking applied to filmmaking years
before the term existed in tech.

### "Disney and Michael Eisner...are going to realize that they actually just created their worst nightmare, a true animation competitor to Disney that can do something that they can't."

Steve saw the post-success negotiation
before the film even shipped. He raised
IPO capital not to celebrate — but to
have leverage in the renegotiation he
knew was coming. Thinking two moves
ahead while everyone else was making
the movie.

### "Pixar alone made Steve Jobs a billionaire on IPO day."

He put in $54M over nine years,
owned 78% on IPO day, walked out
with ~$1.2B. Not Apple — Pixar.
Most people get this wrong about
Jobs's personal wealth.

### "Steve and Pixar did get a reality check, and the reality check is that they don't need Disney at all."

The memo backfired completely. Eisner wanted
leverage; instead he handed Jobs the evidence to
walk. Classic negotiating **own goal**.

### "After 10 months of trying to strike a deal with Disney, we're moving on. We've had a great run together, one of the most successful in Hollywood history, and it's a shame that Disney won't be participating in Pixar's future successes."

Jobs wrote this himself.
The passive-aggressive closer — "shame that Disney
won't be participating" — is a **masterclass** in
public negotiation via press release.

### "It was so unthinkable and such a surprise to the whole board that they forgot to say no."

Iger's Pixar pitch was so audacious the board
rubber-stamped it by accident. That's not a
governance story — it's a signal of how **broken**
Disney's confidence was by then.

### "My cancer is back, and I don't know how much longer I'm gonna live."

Jobs disclosed his cancer to Iger on the morning
of the announcement, 30 minutes before it went
public, and offered him a way out. The real
reason Pixar sold wasn't business strategy —
it was **mortality**.

### "ESPN paid for Pixar, Marvel, and Lucasfilm."

All three acquisitions cost roughly four years
of ESPN cable profits. The **Buffett framing**
Ben reaches for here is exactly right: ESPN was
the cash cow that funded every creative bet.

## ESPN Breaks Disney, Cord-Cutting Begins

### "Disney's stock price today is what it was then. It's gone up and down, but it is flat to 11 years ago."

Against a 3.5× S&P 500 over the same period,
Disney is a decade of lost compounding.
All the acquisition genius, all the IP —
**none of it** translated into shareholder returns
once the cable geyser stopped.

### "Disney used to operate in an unbelievable and easy environment. The cable bundle allowed ESPN to generate huge profits, basically on autopilot."

The argument in two sentences. The whole renaissance wasn't just smart management — it was **tailwinds mistaken for skill**. Worth remembering when evaluating any business's historic returns.

### "Streaming turns out to be expensive to build, expensive to maintain, expensive to acquire customers, and harder to create events around."

The structural reversal is total. Every advantage flips: theatrical events become subscriptions nobody rallies around, and retention costs eat the margin. The old model printed money passively; the new one burns it actively.

### "The prosperity from the late '90s and the 2005 to 2019 era, that was a complete anomaly due to how good all the structural forces around the business were."

Disney will always be **benchmarked against a freak period**. That's the trap — the comparison set is unfair by design. Any analysis of Disney's "decline" needs to start here.

## Streaming's Structural Mismatch

### "once Netflix's algorithm controls whether families see your movies or not, you've got a big problem"

The distribution dependency argument in one sentence. Losing algorithmic control is existential — not a trade-off, a slow death. This is the cleaner version of the "build vs. buy distribution" debate that applies directly to any platform-dependent business, including horse racing data feeds.

### "it's crazy to think that Disney didn't have any relationship with any of their customers prior to this unless you had visited a park"

For 100 years Disney routed through intermediaries — retailers, theatres, cable operators — and collected none of the customer data. A century of compounding brand value with zero first-party data. The streaming bet was partly a data acquisition play dressed up as a content play.

### "that whole strategy of a firehose of content is completely orthogonal to the flywheel strategy of only the best content very infrequently"

Disney's historical model was infrequent theatrical releases treated as cultural moments, then quiet harvesting — the exact opposite of what streaming requires. This is the central strategic contradiction of the whole episode. Two business models that cannot coexist in the same content pipeline.

### "the Netflix brand doesn't mean anything to anyone. The Netflix brand is the button that I push on my TV when I wanna watch stuff"

Disney's brand carries accumulated trust that Netflix's doesn't — meaning a run of bad releases damages Disney's compounding competitive advantage in a way that bad Netflix content simply doesn't. Netflix can absorb mediocrity. Disney cannot. The brand is both the asset and the vulnerability.

### "you don't get a second exploitation window the way that a limited edition home video re-release has"

Theatrical re-releases followed by home video windows were structurally superior — multiple revenue events from a single piece of content. Streaming collapses all exploitation windows into one perpetual low-margin subscription. The unit economics never recovered from that structural change.

### "streaming wasn't just like invest some money to build it out one time and then we've got this great asset. It's this constant, expensive nurturing thing"

By 2023-24 Disney was confronting the reality that streaming is an ongoing operational burden — customer acquisition, churn management, tech investment — not a one-time capital deployment. The capex framing fooled a lot of smart people. Streaming is opex-heavy forever, not a build-then-harvest model.

### "the winning streaming service will be the kitchen sink"

The brutal logic here: scale streaming requires broad content, broad content requires diverse audiences, diverse audiences require everything. Disney built a boutique brand and then had to abandon it the moment they launched a streaming service.

### "Disney+ doesn't actually give you the reach that being on the biggest streaming services would give you"

130M vs 350M subscribers — that's the reach gap Disney accepted to own its distribution. The irony is that theatrical gave Disney *universal* reach; Disney+ gives it a walled garden with 40% of the audience.

## The Franchise Pipeline Thins

### "the three acquisitions, Marvel, Pixar, Lucasfilm, were brilliant. But in retrospect, it's kind of looking like they provided amazing fuel for about 20 years, but not for 50 years."

This is the sharpest bear case in the episode. The acquisitions looked like permanent IP moats; they may turn out to be finite fuel tanks. When Marvel fatigue and Star Wars exhaustion land at the same time, what's the next move?

### "Has Disney produced a single new franchise in the last decade that has been a commercial success?"

Everything post-2016 that made serious money is a sequel or legacy IP harvest. Coco, Encanto, Elemental are culturally real — but none of them broke box office records, which means streaming has to carry the franchise-building work now.

### "In the long run, I think these franchises... are just like luxury brands. You cannot kill them."

The bull case in one sentence. It's not wrong — Star Wars survived the Holiday Special, Marvel survived the '90s bankruptcy. But "can't be killed" and "reliably generates 20% returns" are very different claims.

## Parks Carry the Company Now

### "parks is now almost 60% of the company's operating income, while ESPN and sports is down to 16%. So it's a total reversal from the situation ten years ago."

The single most important structural fact in this excerpt. Disney spent a decade being an ESPN/cable business and is now a **theme park business** that happens to stream. The CEO appointment follows the money perfectly.

### "Disney's best bet here is to be a clear number 2."

Chasing number one means the **content kitchen sink** — spiralling spend to match Netflix's scale, which Disney can't win on unit economics alone. Accepting number two is strategically honest; most companies can't stomach it.

### "Absent a completely different business model, like being MrBeast, there's a whole other universe where you can create very successful media business models now, but it's so far far from Disney's universe that you can't even see a path to it."

The **creator economy** is a genuine alternative media empire — just not one Disney can pivot into. Brutal framing. The exits that exist aren't exits Disney can use.

### "Disney is the home of generational myths. And you'll never kill it."

The optimist counter: IP that embeds itself in **childhood memory** has a floor that business fundamentals can't fully erode. The '90s may never return, but neither does Disney disappear. Whether that floor is investable is a different question.

## Key Arguments

1. **Creative collapse is structural, not cyclical**
   Animation neglected for 18 years left parks
   and licensing carrying everything —
   a warning sign repeated in the streaming era.

2. **The Broadway-musical insight** unlocked
   the Renaissance — one "I want" song,
   third scene, built audience empathy
   and turned cartoons into billion-dollar franchises.

3. **ESPN was an accident** that became
   a Buffett-style cash cow:
   locked-in carriage fees, 20% annual
   rate rises, zero substitutes —
   and funded all three mega-acquisitions.

4. **Pixar was built on compounding accidents**
   (Lasseter fired, Lucas divorcing, Jobs ousted);
   Jobs's IPO timing the week Toy Story opened
   reset the power balance from supplier to equal.

5. **ESPN also broke Disney** — splitting it
   into two incompatible businesses, enabling
   the Eisner ouster, and masking the
   cord-cutting cliff until it was too late.

6. **Streaming is structurally wrong** for Disney's
   moat — quality IP erodes when you produce
   at Netflix volume; every mediocre sequel
   shrinks the brand premium that justified the model.

7. **Parks are the real business now** (~60%
   of operating income); the franchise pipeline
   is thinning with no new IP since ~2016 —
   Disney is living off 15-20 year old acquisitions.
