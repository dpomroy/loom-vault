# Notes: #427 How Raymond Plank Built a $50 Billion Oil Company

## Summary

Raymond Plank built Apache Corporation from
**$250k to $50 billion** over 50 years.

His memoir, written at 90, isn't a company
history — it's a **philosophy manual**.

Key ideas: cost obsession beats talent,
ignorance of "how it's done" is an edge,
and patience lets someone else's invention
make your old assets valuable again.

Plank was shaped early — dead mother at 15,
combat pilot at 22, depression-era father.
Those experiences made business risk feel
trivial by comparison.

For Daniel: the counterposition strategy
(buying what majors ignore), the "stay in
the game until tech makes your assets
valuable" idea, and the co-founder values
alignment lesson are the most transferable
signals here.

## Quotes

### "We did not feel qualified. We proceeded nonetheless."

The only two sentences that matter in the
whole origin story. **Competence follows
commitment** — you don't need the domain
knowledge to start, you need the edge no
one else has (his was finance and tax).

### "Beaten paths are for beaten men."

The full context makes this land harder:
"what I did not know has been a tremendous
advantage... I had not fallen victim to how
others learned, knew and practised."
**Ignorance as moat** — not despite the
lack of expertise, because of it.

### "Ego can quickly outstrip one's competence and fat heads get flattened."

The co-founder literally **bugged the
offices**. Plank frames the lesson as ego
destroying competence, not as a personal
betrayal. That's the right frame — and
worth keeping when hiring or partnering.

### "Values are not created equal. My time horizons for Apache were long term. Truman was impatient in his personal drive to become a high roller."

The cleanest **co-founder selection
filter** in the whole episode: not skill,
not even values in the abstract — it's
*time horizon*. Build vs. extract. That
question surfaces everything.

### "I wrote a daily report on my activities that contributed to the basis for confidence and support from the board."

Started during a crisis, kept going when
things were fine. **Over-communication as
trust infrastructure** — boards don't panic
when they're never surprised. Simple and
almost nobody actually does it.

### "If you stay in the game long enough to get lucky, someone else will then come along and invent technology that make your assets more valuable."

Applied directly to Apache: wells bought
in the 70s that looked spent started
**yielding again 40 years later** because
of new drilling techniques invented by
others. Patience is leverage — if you
own the asset, you capture the upside
from inventions you didn't make.

### "We were a bit like pigs following cows through a cornfield. The scraps are pretty good for a company with our particular strategy."

The majors *wanted* to sell — they were
chasing larger reserves and cutting costs
on depleting fields. **Counterposition
only works if the other side's incentives
push them out** of the space you want.
Plank understood that clearly.

### "I believe immense job creation would be driven from the expanding roots of technological innovations and deferred availability of goods and services."

He was **contrarian at the macro level**
when most people feared another depression
post-WWII. That macro bet preceded
everything — the accounting firm, the oil
pivot, all of it. The whole $50B starts
with one correct non-consensus view.

### "One trade off in my pursuits was my family who needed and warranted more of my time, love, attention and support."

Rare to see a founder this **honest about
the cost without either excusing it or
drowning in guilt**. "Life is to live,
learn and grow" — he's not romanticising
the tradeoff, just refusing to
pretend it wasn't one.

### "Most markets in my experience, rarely pause and stabilize where rationality might suggest that they should."

Seven decades of market observation
compressed into one sentence. **Markets
overshoot** — oil, trading, anything
cyclical. Plank built Apache to survive
the irrational troughs, not just profit
from the peaks. Directly relevant to
the trading pipeline.

## Key Arguments

1. **Ignorance of industry norms is an
edge**, not a liability — Plank entered
oil with zero technical knowledge but
superior finance/tax skills, and that
asymmetry became Apache's founding moat.

2. **Cost obsession compounds** — controlling
every dollar wasn't frugality for its own
sake; it let Apache survive cyclical
downturns that killed competitors and
pursue deals others couldn't afford.

3. **Counterposition over competition** —
buying assets the majors were incentivised
to discard ("pigs following cows") meant
Apache never had to beat anyone directly;
it occupied ignored space.

4. **Time horizon is the deepest values
test** for partners and co-founders — the
Truman conflict wasn't about ethics, it was
build-long vs. cash-out-fast; that single
divergence destroyed the partnership.

5. **Staying in the game is a strategy** —
long-held assets can be re-valued by
technology you didn't invent; patience
plus ownership is a compounding position
that requires no additional insight.
