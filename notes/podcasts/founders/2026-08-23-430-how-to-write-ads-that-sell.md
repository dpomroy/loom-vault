# Notes: #430 How to Write Ads That Sell

## Summary

**Advertising is salesmanship in print** — that is Claude Hopkins' central claim, and every idea in this episode flows from it.

Hopkins believed output volume compounds faster than talent. He credited his success not to ability but to exceptional hours, and he traced that work ethic to poverty forcing him into experience rather than theory. The discipline is the point.

The enemy of good advertising is personal taste. Hopkins was blunt: what you like is probably irrelevant, because you are not the public. The only court that matters is actual buyers, and the only honest test is whether the ad sells.

The **Schlitz Beer case** is the episode's anchor. Hopkins didn't invent a new brewing process — he simply told the story of the one that already existed, before any competitor thought to. First to tell the story wins the narrative, even when the product is identical.

From there the episode builds toward the role of research. The winning insight — the "keystone fact" — is almost always buried in material most people won't bother reading. The simple, clean ad you see at the end is the *output* of that obsessive, unglamorous preparation. Simplicity isn't the starting point; it's the reward for doing the work.

The episode closes on a quiet warning: lead with the reward, not the risk avoided. People act toward beauty and gain; they don't act to prevent disaster. If your best promise isn't in the headline, the ad has already failed. Genius, Hopkins said, is the art of taking pains.

## Work Ethic Over Raw Talent

### "If I have gone higher than others in advertising or done more, the fact is not due to exceptional ability but to exceptional hours." [5:30]

The man who works twice as long learns twice as much — more mistakes, more successes, more reps. The ability vs. volume debate ends here. Hopkins is unambiguous: volume wins. Relevant to anything you're compounding — trading models, Loom, the racing tool.

### "To poverty I owe the fact that I never went to college. I spent those four years in the school of experience instead of the school of theory." [6:48]

Hopkins frames this not as a loss but as an advantage — four years of real feedback loops over four years of abstraction. The framing matters: constraints as advantages, not excuses. Classic INTJ reframe.

### "Which is work and which is play? If a thing is useful, they call it work. If it's useless, they call it play. But one is hard as the other. All the difference I see lies in the attitude of the mind." [9:08]

The railroad foreman made work a game while his helpers counted hours to quitting time. This isn't motivational fluff — it's a **productivity arbitrage**. The person who enjoys the work gets more reps in the same clock time.

## Know the Public, Not Yourself

### "We must never judge humanity by ourselves. The things we want, the things we like may appeal to a small minority." [15:00]

Only the "obstinate and boneheaded" rely on personal preference — everything must go to the court of public opinion. This is the anti-founder-ego principle. **Test, don't assume.** Directly applicable to Loom's feed curation — what Daniel finds compelling isn't the signal.

### "The average salesman seeks favors. He seeks profit for himself. His plea is, 'Buy my goods.' He makes a selfish appeal to selfish people, and of course, he meets resistance. I was selling service." [26:35]

Hopkins' whole basis was helping the buyer get more business — his own advantage was buried inside his effort to please them. He considered advertising simply salesmanship in print. Every bad ad in the wild violates this. **Lead with what you do for them**, not what you are.

## The Schlitz Lesson: Tell the Story First

### "I told a story common to all good brewers, but a story which had never been told."

The Schlitz case in one line. Hopkins wrote nothing proprietary — just the process everyone used — and Schlitz went from fifth to first. The lesson: **familiarity to you ≠ familiarity to your customer**. Your "boring" operations are someone else's marvel.

### "The maker is too close to his product. He sees in his methods only the ordinary."

This is the trap every founder falls into. What feels like table-stakes internally is often genuinely impressive to outsiders. Relevant to Loom: the infrastructure and reasoning behind features probably looks mundane to Daniel but could be a **genuine differentiator** if surfaced clearly.

## Samples and Service Beat Arguments

### "A good product is its own best salesman. It is uphill work to sell goods without samples." [13:05]

Hopkins learned this at age 10 selling his mother's silver polish door-to-door: talking about it converted 1-in-10, demonstrating it converted nearly all. Estee Lauder figured out the same thing independently. If you believe in your product, give it away first — the conversion math works.

### "Ask a person to take a chance on you and you have a fight. Offer to take a chance on him, and the way is easy."

One of Hopkins' cleanest reversals. The framing shift is small; the commercial effect is enormous. Most copy asks the customer to **bear the risk** — great copy absorbs it instead.

## Sell the Reward, Not the Risk

### "People will do anything to cure trouble, but little to prevent it."

The painkillers-not-vitamins principle, 100 years before it became a startup cliché. Hopkins proved it by selling Pepsodent as a **beauty product**, not a decay-prevention product — and made himself a million dollars doing it.

## Headlines Carry the Whole Promise

### "The identical ad run with various headlines differs tremendously in its returns. It's not uncommon for a change in headlines to multiply returns from five to ten times over."

Five to ten times — from one variable. Hopkins obsessed over headlines accordingly, spending hours on a single one. Most people spend 90% of their time on body copy; the **leverage is inverted**.

### "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck."

Be specific; use real numbers. "Best in the world" signals nothing. "My net profit is 3%" is **immediately credible** because it's verifiable and precise. Vague superlatives are free to say, so readers discount them to zero.

### "Your main appeal should lie in your headline."

The entire product promise — distilled
to one line. Hopkins and Jobs landed
on the same principle decades apart.
**1000 songs in your pocket** proves
the rule still holds.

## Research as the Hidden Variable

### "One fact may be the keystone of his success to advertise a toothpaste."

The writer read volumes of dry
scientific material and found a single
idea buried mid-book — one sentence
that made millions for the client.

The ratio is brutal: weeks of input,
**one usable output**. Most people
quit before they find it.

### "The ad seems so simple, and it must be simple to appeal. But back of that ad may lie reams of data, volumes of information and months of research."

Simplicity is the *output* — not the
process. Readers see the iceberg
tip. The **months underneath** are
what make it float.

### "This is no lazy man's field."

Four words. Full stop.

Hopkins ends the book where he started
— effort is the **only non-negotiable**.
Talent without research doesn't compete.

## Genius Is Taking Pains

### "Genius is the art of taking pains."

Hopkins repeats this twice — which
is itself a signal. It's not
about being clever. It's about
**not stopping** when the material
gets boring.

### "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in this new Rolls Royce comes from the electric clock."

Ogilvy found this line in an obscure
engineering document — not written
for public consumption — while reading
hundreds of pages about Rolls Royce.
It became one of his most successful
campaigns ever.

The line does zero selling. That's
**exactly why it sells**.

## Key Arguments

1. **Output volume compounds faster than talent**
   — exceptional hours over decades beat raw
   ability; more work = more mistakes + more wins

2. **Never trust your own taste**
   — personal preference is the enemy of good ads;
   the public is the only court that matters

3. **Demonstration beats argument**
   — a free sample or live proof converts
   far better than any written claim

4. **Tell your process, even if it's standard**
   — competitors' silence gives you
   first-mover advantage in *narrative*,
   not product (the Schlitz lesson)

5. **Sell the reward, not the risk avoided**
   — humans are present-biased; lead with
   beauty and success, not disaster averted

6. **The headline carries the main promise**
   — if the best benefit isn't in the headline,
   the ad has already failed

7. **Research depth determines quality**
   — the keystone idea is almost always buried
   in material most people wouldn't bother reading;
   simplicity is the *output* of obsessive prep
