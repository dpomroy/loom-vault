# Notes: How Michael Bloomberg Works

## Summary

Bloomberg's core thesis is simple: **outwork** everyone, and results follow.

Fired at 39 with a $10M severance from Salomon Brothers, Michael Bloomberg didn't write a business plan. He built a product first, hired salespeople immediately, and figured out the rest later. His operating theory was explicit — volume of effort beats strategic intelligence, every time.

His edge wasn't genius. It was **calculated indispensability**. He arrived first, left last, and positioned himself close to the people who made decisions. He never understood why others didn't do the same thing.

Every major advance he made was evolutionary, not revolutionary. Small earned steps, compounding over time. He was suspicious of banks and venture capitalists precisely because they inject doubt and slow that compounding down.

The media empire — news, radio, television — was never the real product. Each news story was a product demo. More demos meant more terminal subscriptions. Bloomberg understood that hardware and broadcast were delivery mechanisms, not businesses. The subscription was always the point.

He named the company after himself deliberately. That personal brand became a distribution advantage that no competitor could easily copy. The goal was never a fair fight — it was to enter every contest already ahead.

## Effort as Explicit Strategy

### "The more you work, the better you do. It's that simple. I've always outworked the other person, and if I hadn't, he or she would be writing this book."

The simplest possible thesis, stated without apology. No framework, no nuance — just output. Most people want this to be more complicated than it is.

### "I never understood why everybody else doesn't do the same thing, make himself indispensable on the job."

He frames this as obvious, not clever. That's the tell — he genuinely can't see why you wouldn't arrive first and leave last. Works as a hiring filter too: people who ask this question are the ones worth keeping.

### "We do not want fair fights. We want to go into contests with an advantage."

Identical framing to Bezos. The pattern holds: the best operators don't compete — they engineer situations where competition is already tilted. Relevant to any market you enter.

## Why Central Planning Fails Founders

### "Don't devise a five year plan or a great leap forward. Central planning didn't work for Stalin or Mao, and it won't work for an entrepreneur either."

The Stalin/Mao comparison is deliberately extreme — which is the point. Bloomberg uses history constantly to make obvious things land harder. Relevant to Loom's own build: stop planning the product you don't have users for yet.

### "Banks and venture capitalists can be the worst enemies of entrepreneurs. They create doubt in entrepreneurs minds."

He's not posturing — he watched a VC nearly tank his early momentum by demanding **structure** where improvisation was the actual advantage. The warning is: outside money often imports outside assumptions about how a business should look.

## Build First, Sell Simultaneously

### "At Bloomberg, we always built the product first. We think about accounting and shipping much later in the process. Selling is the only process we run simultaneously with development from the start."

Clean operational principle. **Product + sales in parallel, everything else after.** No HR strategy, no finance system, no org chart — until you have something people actually pay for. Directly applicable to the horse racing tool and Loom.

### "Every significant advance I or my company's ever made has been evolutionary rather than revolutionary. Small earned steps, not big lucky hits."

This contradicts how Bloomberg is usually framed — as a disruptor who blindsided Bloomberg. His own account is the opposite: relentless **incremental compounding**. The terminal wasn't a moonshot; it was a fixable bug list that never stopped getting fixed.

## Media as Product Demo

### "Each news story is a product demo. More demos lead to more revenue."

The cleanest expression of Bloomberg's flywheel. Most media companies think of content as the business — Bloomberg used it as a funnel. That's a fundamentally different mental model.

### "I would become the Colonel Sanders of Financial Information Services."

Absurd comparison, but tactically correct. He identified that his competitors' founders were dead and he was alive and loud — that asymmetry was a real competitive edge. Personal brand as distribution moat.

## Never Confuse Delivery with Business

### "We never made the mistake of believing that we were in the hardware business. We are in the business of producing and distributing the world's most accurate, reliable, comprehensive, up to the second information and analysis."

Knowing what business you're actually in is rarer than it sounds. Most founders get captured by their current delivery mechanism. Bloomberg built computers and didn't mistake themselves for a hardware company — worth stress-testing against any product you're building.

### "It occurred to me, one of us is stupid and it isn't him."

The willingness to flip a hard "no" into a hire, same day, is the thing. No ego protection, no rationalising the prior position. Rare.

## Accountability and Excuses

### "No one's got an excuse, but everyone's got a reason. This is maddening."

The most honest description of organisational decay I've seen in a while. The "why we can't" creep is the thing that kills the builder mentality — and Bloomberg names it directly rather than pretending it doesn't happen at scale.
