# Notes: Luca Ferrari on Building Bending Spoons: Extreme Ownership, Talent Science & Relentless Simplification

## Summary

**Bending Spoons** has turned software acquisition into a repeatable science by solving one problem first: people.

Luca Ferrari's core belief is that raw cognitive ability and drive outlast experience every time. Experience can even degrade talent — exposure to low standards normalises them. So Bending Spoons built a dedicated talent team, removed hiring decisions from managers (who have all the wrong incentives), and aggregates over 100 weak signals to predict long-term potential. They are looking for brains and hunger, not résumés.

The second load-bearing idea is **extreme ownership** — not as a slogan, but as something detectable in someone's history. It shows up in how candidates treated the receptionist, in academic records, in personal projects. Past a certain intelligence threshold, this quality separates the elite from the merely smart.

Once the right people are in, the operating principle is ruthless subtraction. Most companies do forty things when only ten are ROI-positive. At Bending Spoons, complexity must justify itself — the burden of proof sits with those who want to add, never with those who want to remove. Complete removal beats incremental reduction almost every time.

Evernote makes the proof legible. Bought for around $200M, staffed down from 350 to 50-60 people, it now generates $100-200M revenue at roughly 55% margins. A general manager fixed in three minutes a bug that would have taken the old organisation weeks. Small, dense teams with genuine ownership simply outwork large ones.

The acquisition model follows the same logic: 99% of resources go into being the best operator, almost nothing into deal-finding. The moat is the operating system, not the deal flow.

## Experience Can Work Against You

### "you can actually get worse through experience. If you're exposed to low standards, for example, of performance, you'll normalize those over time" [0:08:44]

This is the sharpest point in the excerpt.
**Normalised mediocrity** is an active liability,
not neutral. A fresh hire with high idealism
can outperform a decade of bad conditioning.

### "talent, meaning a good brain and a massive eagerness to excel, grow, make an impact. Those never fail to be valuable." [0:08:44]

Experience decays; **raw drive compounds**.
The practical implication: if you can give
someone experience (challenges, great colleagues),
you only need to hire for the thing you can't give.

### "Most experts I've talked to believe he wasn't even probably a top 50 talent in his generation, but went down as one of the three best, for sure." [0:56:54]

Nadal as proof-of-concept for the whole thesis.
**Raw talent has a ceiling effect** — past a
competence threshold, desire separates the
outliers. Worth noting Luca uses this with
new joiners directly.

## Hiring as Signal Aggregation

### "we've really built a science out of studying people's track records, including academic records and personal projects and similar things whereby in Samos application we identify over 100 different signals and through those we predict their long term potential." [0:15:09]

The quant-fund analogy is the right frame.
No single signal is decisive — **aggregate
weak signals** beat strong-but-sparse gut reads.
Directly relevant to how I'd think about
feature engineering in a scoring model.

### "hiring managers, meaning that that person who runs the team, have almost all the wrong incentives in hiring." [0:22:36]

They feel the pain of the empty seat, so they
fill it fast with **"good enough."**
That's rational for them; catastrophic for
the organisation. Classic principal-agent failure.

### "setting highly specific concrete objectives to which career progression or pay are tied almost invariably leads to bad outcomes" [0:25:17]

No bonuses, no KPI-linked pay.
The argument: **intrinsic motivation + trust**
outperforms mechanical incentives, which just
optimise the metric rather than the outcome.
Goodhart's Law, basically — he just doesn't
name it that.

## Extreme Ownership Defined

### "Extreme ownership is caring in your belly tremendously about being the best at what you do, about helping the team and the company succeed." [0:50:45]

Luca's version differs from Jocko's — it's less
about accountability and more about **raw desire**.
The distinction matters: one is a behaviour,
the other is a personality trait you can't
really install.

### "there might be proof that small teams of very capable people with extreme ownership really care, can do, can outwork and out produce vast organizations" [1:36:39]

This is the central bet Bending Spoons is making operationally. The Instagram/WhatsApp examples are the receipts — not theory. Directly relevant if you're ever tempted to hire your way out of a problem on Loom.

### "if you want to, we encourage you to go and shop it around because, in fact, once you convince yourself that this is the best offer, it'll be easier for us too" [1:53:39]

They actively **invite** sellers to find a better deal, knowing they won't. This is a confidence play that only works if the underlying advantage is real. If you have to hustle to close, your offer is weak — and you know it.

## Stripping Work to ROI-Positive

### "I think most companies do things that are ROI negative. There is 100 things they could be doing, but only 10 are ROI positive. Many companies are doing 40 things." [0:30:54]

The framing is brutal and probably accurate.
Most companies aren't choosing between good options —
they're **drowning in noise** and calling it strategy.

### "The burden of proof is on those making that suggestion. The people who support the thesis that we shouldn't be adding that complexity don't need to prove it." [0:59:28]

Flipping the default is the key move here.
Most orgs require you to **argue against** new
features/processes — Bending Spoons requires you
to argue for them. One rule change, big cultural
shift.

### "Everybody at the company, certainly the people who have shown promise, they should have way more on their plate than feels even remotely comfortable." [0:43:07]

The logic is mathematical, not motivational.
**More tasks = larger selection pool**, so
expected ROI of chosen work goes up.
It's also a filter: people who can't handle
the volume don't get promoted.

### "Once you become really good at being comfortable with having 100 times more things you could be doing than you can actually do, that's an insane superpower to have." [0:43:07]

This is the actual skill being trained — not
work ethic, but **comfort with incompleteness**.
Most people never develop it because their
managers protect them from the feeling.

## Simplification as Operating Principle

### "often, by far, the biggest wins in terms of simplification is complete removal" [1:04:49]

The biggest wins come from complete removal. This is the thing most product teams never do — they optimise incrementally and congratulate themselves. Relevant to Loom: what's in the product right now that should just be gone?

### "we just got rid of titles and we told people, you can pick your title for your CV, LinkedIn, whatever. We don't need to know, we don't want to know." [1:04:49]

They removed job titles entirely and told people to self-assign for external purposes — the company opted out of the whole system. The logic is airtight: titles serve the employee's ego and CV, not the company — so let the employee own it. Zero downside, massive cognitive overhead eliminated.

### "you want to beat complexity with talent, not process, and keep rules to a minimum" [1:38:01]

Ferrari credits this partly to Netflix's culture deck. One of the cleanest formulations of the anti-bureaucracy thesis I've seen. Worth posting somewhere visible.

## The Bending Spoons Operating System

### "we have invested pretty heavily at Bending Spoons over this point over a decade to develop basically you could look at it as an operating system at this point. Over 50 proprietary tools that run almost everything that we do" [1:12:54]

Bending Spoons has spent a decade building 50+ proprietary tools that function as an internal operating system. This is the real moat — not the acquisitions themselves, but the platform they're installed onto. Every new acquisition improves the OS; the OS makes the next acquisition cheaper to run.

### "she, general manager for Evernote, in maybe three minutes, essentially identified and fixed a bug. Something that would have taken...weeks, maybe forever." [1:33:15]

The GM of Evernote used their internal AI agent (AltSpooner) to identify a bug, check support data, find root causes in the codebase, propose a fix, and ping the lead engineer — all in ~3 minutes. Human-to-human, that's weeks. This is the clearest concrete example of what genuine AI integration looks like operationally — not copilot suggestions, but autonomous multi-system coordination.

### "we probably focus 99% of our resources and efforts in being the best operator... and remarkably little in the acquisition side of things" [1:48:38]

Operational excellence is the **primary lever** — acquisitions become easy once the platform is powerful enough. This inverts the typical PE playbook entirely. Most buyers compete on deal-sourcing; these guys compete on what happens after.

## The Evernote Transformation

### "if you're running Evernote and that's all you do...you make the change I just described in terms of headcount, if something goes wrong, you're out of a job" [1:24:22]

A standalone CEO can't make radical headcount cuts because failure ends their career — the blemish never clears. Portfolio structure removes that incentive trap. Being part of a larger whole lets you take bets that are correct in expected value but too risky for someone whose identity is tied to one outcome.

### "we rebuilt the org...it was roughly 350 team members...a year, a year and a half after the acquisition closed. We were more around 50 to 60 team members." [1:23:11]

Post-acquisition, Bending Spoons cut Evernote from ~350 to 50–60 people within 18 months. And now it's 20. That's not restructuring — that's a different theory of how software businesses should be staffed. The interesting question is whether AI made the last cut from 50 to 20 possible, or whether that was always the target.

### "if you look at the timeline of say product improvements features before we acquired it in the, say two, three years before and after it's night and day" [1:34:59]

Evernote moving 3x faster with a smaller team post-acquisition. "Despite" is wrong — it's *because*. The bloat was the bottleneck, not the headcount gap.

## Patience, Decisions and Delay

### "procrastination is awesome if it doesn't come from laziness, because if you postpone decisions, you often have more information when you actually get to make them" [1:44:27]

Ferrari's explicit framing of **deliberate delay** as a decision-making tool. The caveat matters — this only works if the delay is active, not avoidance. Useful discipline for the trading pipeline decisions I keep deferring without a framework.

## Key Arguments

1. **Experience is overrated** — raw cognitive
   ability + drive beats résumé depth;
   bad environments actively degrade talent,
   so the signal decays fast.

2. **Centralise hiring** — managers optimise
   to fill seats quickly; a dedicated talent
   team with cross-portfolio data holds the
   bar categorically better.

3. **Hiring = signal aggregation** — 100+ weak
   predictors (test scores, how candidates
   treat staff, academic record) reliably
   surface long-term potential even with
   near-zero track record.

4. **Extreme ownership** beats intelligence
   past a threshold — detectable in someone's
   history of grinding through failure,
   not in their stated ambition.

5. **Complexity costs are nonlinear** — the
   default must flip: complexity needs
   *justification*, not acceptance;
   complete removal beats incremental reduction.

6. **Talent density is the acquisition edge** —
   legacy brands can't attract hungry people;
   Bending Spoons can, so rotating staff
   into acquisitions spreads culture and
   prevents stagnation.

7. **Delay critical decisions deliberately** —
   waiting until the last responsible moment
   is information accumulation, not indecision;
   applied even to IPO timing.
