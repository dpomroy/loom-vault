# Notes: Doug Leone on Sequoia, Fear, Great Founders & Starting Over at 69

## Summary

Doug Leone — former Sequoia managing partner,
now back at 69 as a self-described analyst —
uses this conversation to unpack what actually
drives elite investing and leadership.

The throughline: **fear over ambition**.
Not vision, not pattern-matching — terror of
irrelevance is what keeps performance high.

He identifies trust (intention + competence)
as the zero-th requirement before anything
else. His edge is EQ — reading people, not
deep tech knowledge. On AI, he's a genuine
believer, not a hype-follower. And on boards,
he's blunt: most VCs are useless, and founders
should treat board construction as a hiring
decision.

## Quotes

### "each time I remade myself, it wasn't really done out of vision of greatness. It was done out of fear of just being gone." [0:00:15]

The honest version of every "I had a vision" founder story. Fear of obsolescence is a cleaner, more reliable engine than ambition — and Leone doesn't dress it up.

### "I feel like I've entered and AI has gotten further away from me than when I first started 90 days ago." [0:00:15]

He's 90 days back in and already behind. That's the real signal about AI's pace — not the hype decks, but a 26-year veteran feeling the ground move under him.

### "I only want the ones where you can return 100 times your money. That's all I'm interested in." [0:23:00]

The 20-investment heuristic forces this conclusion. Most people intellectually agree with outlier-hunting but still fill their portfolio with 3x bets. Leone's framing makes the discipline structural, not willpower.

### "every venture investor has made the mistake of selling too early. Their winners, every single venture investors." [0:23:53]

Sequoia owned 20% of Nvidia and 10% of Google — and still sold too soon. The lesson isn't to hold everything; it's that when you find something with an **unbounded market**, the correct position size is "never sell."

### "I'm interested in the business and the building the business of a company, not in shaping the technology of a company." [0:25:50]

Clean separation of roles. The founder owns the product; the investor owns the go-to-market scaffolding. Crossing that line is where most board members cause damage.

### "I viewed it more as a psychological assistance call than a call on what to do next." [0:28:28]

The Nubank call from the men's room. Velez knew what to do — he needed **someone in his corner**. Leone's edge isn't information delivery, it's being the one person who picks up when a founder is alone at zero.

### "I'm always a little unhappy. My instincts are to be unhappy, and I carry a positive vibe and people see me as fun to be around. It's not an act." [0:37:09]

Leone describes chronic private misery beneath a genuinely warm exterior. This maps directly onto David Senra's framing of "divine discontent" — the idea that the outer energy and the inner restlessness are *both* real, not contradictions.

### "A word I've always hated that I've learned to like is content. It felt like settling for something." [0:38:10]

Leone hated that word until two or three years ago — it felt like settling. Worth sitting with: if your **default resistance** to contentment is what drives output, ageing into it is both healthy and a little dangerous.

### "If you're willing to do that to yourself, people will follow you and people will torture themselves all the way until the goal's back." [0:36:43]

Leone's point: self-imposed pressure only works as leadership fuel if people can *see* you doing it to yourself. Jensen Huang is the obvious reference point here — it's not the demand that earns loyalty, it's the visible **self-demand**.

### "Trust is interesting, though, because it has both knowledge and intention... I can trust your intention, but I think you're a putz." [0:54:46]

Leone breaks trust into two components: skill and intention. Either one missing means no real trust — you either run or ignore them. This is genuinely useful as an **operating framework** — most trust failures are diagnosable as one component, not both.

### "It doesn't start with trust. It always starts with fear." [0:52:31]

Leone says founders fear VCs by default — the big bad venture guys who want to own everything. Trust is **earned incrementally** — help them in a jam, don't turn the screws when they're down. Realistic timeline: about a year.

### "I happen to think I'm a tip top of my game. But in this one, I have to show it. I haven't shown it yet. And it's all in performance." [0:39:11]

Leone separates self-belief from proof — opinion of yourself counts for nothing until the results are on paper. At 69, rebuilding outside Sequoia, he's applying the **same standard** to himself that he'd apply to any founder on day 91.

### "Choose to architect your board the same way you architect your product." [1:06:09]

The contrast he reaches for says everything — most founders treat a multi-year governance relationship with the same rigour as a Friday night plan. If you'd prototype your product, you should prototype your cap table. Most don't.

### "Maybe 30% are incompetent… another 40%, I call them no ops… maybe 20% know what they're doing." [1:07:34]

A blunt distribution from someone who has sat across the table from hundreds of VCs. The "no ops" category is the underrated danger — not actively harmful, just **useless weight** on a board.

### "You don't really have the board member there. You have the shadow, you have the interpreter, what he thinks he or she thinks a firm wants." [1:09:19]

The partner-politics overlay is the thing founders never see coming. Your board member is **optimising for their own firm**, not your company — and that conflict is structural, not personal.

### "It's not just giving it. It's giving it in the right way, at the right time, with the right tone, with the right words, with examples and so on." [1:11:53]

Leone frames honest feedback as an **execution problem**, not a courage problem. The implication: most people who claim to give hard feedback are actually just being blunt and calling it honesty.

### "Moritz taught me how to listen to the exact word that said, the timing of that word — you really can understand about a human being by just listening to the adjective." [1:18:29]

This is a legitimate evaluative edge, not a soft skill. How someone uses "I" versus "we" is a **tells-more-than-a-deck** signal when you're betting on a person before they have a product.

### "Mike had the ability to dream broadly in ways that I've never seen before. He could answer the question better than anybody I've ever known — what if everything goes right?" [1:21:40]

The specific framing matters — not "what's our downside hedge" but **"what does this look like if nothing goes wrong."** That's a different cognitive posture, and Leone credits it as Moritz's defining edge as an investor.

## Key Arguments

1. **Fear as engine** — Leone frames chronic
   dissatisfaction and terror of irrelevance
   as features, not flaws; the discomfort
   *is* the performance mechanism

2. **Trust has two components** — good
   intentions alone aren't enough; you also
   need genuine **competence** or trust
   breaks under pressure

3. **EQ over pattern-matching** — his core
   edge is reading rooms and people; tech
   knowledge is secondary to the "sniffer"

4. **Board construction = hiring** — founders
   who take the first term sheet optimise
   for the wrong thing; most VCs are either
   incompetent or politically captured

5. **Hard feedback is a craft** — timing,
   tone, and framing determine whether
   feedback lands or just kills the
   relationship

6. **AI is a genuine step-change** — not
   hype; Leone applies strict 100x filters
   and still believes, which carries weight
   given his scepticism baseline
