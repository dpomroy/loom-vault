# Notes: Building Defense Technologies to Protect Democracies | Torsten Reil, Helsing

## Summary

Helsing exists because Torsten Reil made a bet that almost nobody else would make: Europe needed its own AI-defence company, and it needed one immediately.

The founding argument is straightforward. **Drones and autonomous mass** have made crewed platforms — tanks, jets, artillery — into findable, killable targets. Ukraine is proving this in real time. The logical response is not better tanks; it is simple, cheap hardware made lethal by software, produced at a scale that overwhelms any adversary.

That software edge is already real. Helsing's AI was tested against expert human pilots in beyond-visual-range air combat — the discipline considered the last human stronghold. The AI won, decisively. The goal, though, is never to use it. **Deterrence** is the actual product: technology so capable that starting a fight becomes too expensive to contemplate.

Torsten's background is the opposite of what you'd expect. He came from biology, then gaming, with no defence-industry peer group telling him what was impossible. He argues that outsider status is a repeatable edge, not a liability.

Internally, Helsing runs on one principle: talent density creates culture. Values are not written on slides — they emerge from who you hire under pressure, and who you remove when they stop performing. Keeping a weak performer because of a deadline is explicitly rejected; a strong hiring pipeline makes that excuse disappear. Torsten screens for one quality above all others: effortlessness — the signal that someone is operating well within their ceiling, not at its edge.

His final provocation is aimed at Europe: the binding constraint is not regulation or capital. It is ambition.

## Why Crewed Platforms Are Obsolete

### "If you get yourself into a tank, your survival rate is very short." [0:00:10]

Reil's framing here is blunt and deliberate. The era of expensive crewed platforms is ending — not theoretically, but observably, in Ukraine, right now. The hardware procurement debates in NATO capitals are already out of date.

### "Those algorithms now are very robust. They work in daylight, at night, in the snow, even with deformed terrain." [0:05:53]

The GPS-jamming problem — which was a serious blocker for autonomous strike drones — is already solved. This matters: the "AI isn't ready" objection to autonomous weapons is collapsing faster than most people realise.

### "If you really want scaled drone usage, they have to fly autonomously." [0:03:14]

The bottleneck isn't hardware — it's the impossibility of training thousands of skilled operators at scale. AI isn't a nice-to-have here; it's the only path to the numbers that matter. Same logic applies anywhere humans are the scaling ceiling.

## Europe's Defence Funding Gap

### "The defense of a country, of a democratic society, was grouped for banks in the same bucket as pornography and gambling and smoking." [0:10:00]

ESG-era banking logic put Helsing in the same risk bucket as vice industries — not because the reasoning held up, but because "weapons are always bad" is a reflexive heuristic. A good reminder that institutional gatekeepers are often enforcing vibes, not ethics.

### "It's just aggression that's missing. Exactly. It's strategic ambitions." [0:21:06]

Reil's diagnosis of European tech stagnation — lack of ambition, not regulation or capital — is the kind of unfashionable take that's probably correct. Regulation is a friction; low ambition is a ceiling. Different problems entirely.

## Helsing's Market Entry

### "We applied for a big program of record... we were the dark horse, but we ended up winning it." [0:23:38]

Having the €100M buffer meant they could absorb the long timelines of government procurement without needing early revenue proof points — and they used that runway to punch far above their weight. Capital as optionality, not just fuel.

### "When you think something is five years away, it's actually usually now two years away." [0:31:14]

The old rule was "five years" that quietly became seven to ten. Autonomous driving is the canonical example. Now the compression is running the other way. Relevant to every timeline assumption in Loom or the trading pipeline — compressing estimates is the correct prior right now.

## AI Beats Humans in Combat

### "That turns out to be bullshit. Actually the AI is much, much better." [0:29:18]

Pilots told Helsing that beyond-visual-range combat was the last domain where human intuition would reign. They were wrong — and Torsten has the Baltic Sea test data to prove it. Contrarian bets only pay off when you have evidence. Helsing ran the experiment before committing.

### "The truth is in the field — it doesn't matter how much you claim that your system works in tests and government tests. We found out it has to work in reality and in combat." [1:07:38]

This became a **core company value**, not from a workshop prompt — from people who'd been to Ukraine and hadn't slept for 48 hours.

Resonates directly with my own rule: never claim something works without verifying it. Same principle, higher stakes.

## Software Absorbs Hardware Complexity

### "You ideally also absorb a lot of the complexity of the hardware that usually would be these exquisite systems that legacy primes will make into your software so you can make the hardware simpler." [0:44:51]

Simpler sensors, wider manufacturing tolerances — the AI recalibrates and compensates in real time. The intelligence moves into software so the physical object can be cheap and repeatable. This is a clean restatement of a principle that applies well beyond defence: offload complexity to software, commoditise the atom.

## Outsider Advantage

### "It would be much harder for us to build Helsing if from the backgrounds had already been in defense companies. It's easier to be an outsider." [1:20:53]

The **outsider advantage** is the common thread — biology, gaming, defence. Not being embedded in the peer group made it easier to ignore bad norms each time.

The pattern is consistent enough to be a genuine insight rather than post-hoc rationalisation.

### "Commentary is entirely worthless and it has no positive contribution. The only thing that it does is I think it drags some people down." [1:21:13]

Social platforms **incentivise negative commentary** over creation. The result is net negative — and founders are underrepresented on those platforms partly because building is already satisfying.

Hard agree. The opportunity cost of commentary is invisible, which is exactly why it's dangerous.

## Hiring as Performance Management

### "It becomes much easier to move people out that are not a good fit for the company if you know your hiring pipeline is so good that that position will be filled almost immediately." [0:52:55]

The two systems — hiring and firing — are load-bearing on each other. A weak pipeline is the hidden reason managers protect underperformers. Most founders treat these as separate problems. Torsten frames them as one flywheel, which is the right frame.

### "Very often the argument of a manager will be, yeah, but if I fire this person now, then we won't be able to hit our deadline because at least they're going to get the job done somehow." [0:52:55]

Slow delivery, attitude problems, cultural drag — managers tolerate all of it because the alternative feels worse. This is how mediocrity compounds silently. Recognisable failure mode. The fix isn't better values — it's a faster pipeline that removes the hostage dynamic.

### "We listen to the managers explain why they give their reports a certain rating and then the manager is held accountable to that rating." [0:52:55]

Calibration drift is the quiet killer in any review system. By forcing managers to justify ratings aloud, Helsing surfaces inconsistency and makes it hard to bury an underperformer with a generous score. Simple mechanism with high leverage — worth lifting directly into any team process.

## Finding Effortless Talent

### "I try to find whether out whether a person is effortless. That's the probably most important person characteristic I look for." [1:01:22]

The full definition: doesn't try to impress, has **deep knowledge**, and is still curious with high agency. All three together = effortlessness.

A useful hiring frame — though "effortless" is exactly what a skilled faker optimises for, as Torsten himself admits three paragraphs later.

### "In all three cases, there were so good at faking the effortlessness and so good at coming up with fake facts that I didn't read through it." [1:02:58]

His **only hiring failures** were people who gamed the exact signal he screens for.

The tell isn't the method — it's that he still backs it. Worth asking whether the method needs a structural check, not just more intuition.

### "The power law in talent is extreme. There are very few 10 out of 10 people, but they make a disproportionate difference to the company." [1:03:28]

Not a cliché — he says they **see it every day**. Worth finding and giving them a home where they can thrive.

The interesting part is what follows: he makes **exceptions** for 10/10s that he wouldn't for others. That's the real policy, and most companies won't say it out loud.
