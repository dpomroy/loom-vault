# Notes: Lulu Cheng Meservey, Founder of Rostra

## Summary

The central claim is that **going direct** — founders speaking to their audience without media intermediaries — is not a stylistic choice but a strategic necessity.

Traditional PR breaks the signal. Each handoff from founder to PR person to journalist to reader strips away intensity and distorts unconventional ideas. If your idea is genuinely new, it will seem wrong to gatekeepers, and their framing will kill it before it reaches anyone who might care.

The fix is a **manifesto**. Not a press release — a vivid picture of a promised land so compelling that people will leave their jobs, their security, and their comfort to go build it. The founder doesn't need charisma. They need unassailable belief and a coherent vision of where things are going.

Beneath the communications argument sits a deeper one: adoption is harder than building. The real bottleneck for new technology is not engineering — it is persuasion. Getting people to understand something, welcome it, and want to work on it is chronically underinvested. And when you do tell the story, put the human at the centre and let the technology stay in the background. Making the tech the protagonist triggers scepticism.

Two final principles round out the episode. First, narrative is not optional — humans fill silence with invented stories, so founders must supply the connective tissue between facts or lose control of how they are read. Second, distinctiveness compounds but only if it stays fixed; switching focus resets compounding to zero. The thing only you can do is the only thing worth doing consistently.

## Why Unconventional Ideas Need Movements

### "you need to build a movement of people to believe something counterintuitive because you are essentially creating something new that if it were obvious, it would have been done" [0:06:31]

The insurgent framing applied to startups. If your idea needs no persuasion it's probably already been done — the gap between "obvious" and "counterintuitive" is exactly where the communications work lives.

The reason most founder comms feels flat: they skip the part where they acknowledge the idea sounds insane.

### "You don't have to have traditional personal charisma... but you have to have intensity of belief in the idea. So the idea has to be fleshed out and coherent, and then your belief in it needs to be unassailable." [0:27:45]

Charisma is a red herring. **Conviction** is the actual variable. This matters because most founders who avoid going direct frame it as a personality problem when it's actually a clarity problem.

### "The person in the middle has to be the most convicted, the most passionate, and have the clearest idea of the promised land." [0:30:17]

The concentric circles model: everything radiates from one **sovereign individual** who has already resolved their own doubt. Caesar charging the bridge. Napoleon crossing it first. Nobody follows someone who looks like they're auditioning their own belief.

## The Case Against Traditional PR

### "traditional PR meant that you would have to filter your message through somebody else's prism... if you're doing something unconventional, far outside the mainstream, unpopular, then you do not want this to be filtered through somebody else's prism." [0:18:56]

The argument isn't anti-media on principle — it's that **distortion increases proportionally** with how unconventional your idea is. Normal companies can survive the filter. Insurgent ones can't.

Sharp framing. Worth internalising for Loom's own positioning work.

### "you lose the intensity along the way because it becomes watered down. So the founder relays the message to their PR person who then puts it into their words... and then it goes out, and then whoever it's reaching is trying to interpret it." [0:24:42]

Every handoff in the chain is a lossy compression step. By the time a contrarian idea reaches its audience through **five intermediaries**, it reads as a press release. This is the structural case for going direct — not ideology, mechanics.

## The Manifesto as Recruitment Weapon

### "The purpose of a manifesto is to make concrete that intensity and paint for people a vision of what the world could be, such that when they see it, they are willing to leave their jobs, families, security, everything to go and make that happen." [0:15:07]

A manifesto isn't marketing copy — it's a recruitment device. And the bar is extreme: people have to feel they *have* to act, not just that the idea is interesting.

Most startup decks fail this test completely. They describe a market, not a cause.

## Adoption Is Harder Than Building

### "building the technology is often not as hard as getting people to use the technology" [0:31:48]

The underrated insight here. Most founders obsess over product-market fit and ignore narrative-market fit entirely. Loom is partially built on this premise — surfacing ideas matters as much as having them.

### "the greatest and most underestimated barrier between technology existing and actually having an impact is people understanding it, welcoming it into their lives and wanting to go work on it" [0:31:48]

She's right that there's a massive asymmetry — tons of engineering effort, almost no commensurate investment in the human persuasion layer. The AI industry is currently paying this tax in real time.

## Communicating Carefully Around Fear

### "don't look happy when you're saying it. Don't be smiling when you say there's going to be a 50% unemployment rate in a year" [0:33:41]

The Eric Schmidt commencement example makes this concrete. It's not about what you say — it's about whether your affect signals you're on the same side as the audience. Obvious in hindsight, routinely ignored.

### "the human story, and then the thing that you're trying to sell is sort of in the background." [1:08:12]

Dana White built MMA by making the *fighters* the story, not the sport. The product was ambient. Directly relevant to how Loom should think about surfacing content — lead with the person, let the idea follow.

### "the utopian stories about tech to have it in the background. Just be ambient" [0:41:58]

Dystopian stories put the tech in the foreground — that's what makes them scary. Utopian stories bury it. The Jetsons frame is a genuinely useful heuristic: if your pitch *sounds* like a pitch, you've already lost.

## Authenticity Over Templates

### "fake things do not turn out beautiful. And I think it's because no one has put that love and care into them" [0:54:10]

She can't explain the mechanism but the empirical observation holds — fake companies don't produce beautiful things. Beauty as a signal of authenticity is a testable claim, and she's essentially right: slop is legible.

### "when you use a template, you are giving up on the possibility that you could be better" [1:02:22]

Her point about AI writing is sharper than the usual "AI makes you lazy" take — it's that you're opting into a shared median output by design. If you're above average, templates are a voluntary handicap. Direct relevance to how Loom handles note generation — the system should amplify the user's thinking, not replace it with averaged prose.

## Narrative Fills the Silence

### "we all have a form of referential mania. We need to believe that things around us are pointing to something." [1:14:33]

The Nabokov story lands the point cleanly: people aren't passive receivers of facts, they're active meaning-makers. This is the strongest idea in the excerpt.

### "It's the space in between the facts that ties them into a story that is our responsibility to fill in." [1:17:04]

The gap between events is where narrative lives — and it's uncontrolled territory unless you deliberately occupy it. Most founders just broadcast facts and wonder why perception drifts.

## Compounding Requires Consistency

### "you can't compound something that changes. The way compounding works is that it has to be a set principle that then compounds." [1:11:25]

Compounding as a metaphor for identity and strategy, not just money. Most founders pivot when pressure mounts — which resets the clock entirely. Consistency IS the advantage.

### "don't get ready, stay ready, and your big fight could happen anytime." [1:10:41]

UFC fighter mindset: opportunity arrives without warning, so readiness is a permanent state, not a sprint before a deadline. Simple. Useful. Not novel — but the fighter context makes it stick.
