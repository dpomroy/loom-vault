# Notes: Zach Dell on Base Power, Energy Abundance & Building a Company for Life

## Summary

**Base Power** has a single counter-positioning insight: home batteries are sold wrong.

Every incumbent sells expensive hardware that sits idle. Zach Dell's company retains ownership of the battery, operates it as a wholesale grid asset, and passes the savings to homeowners as a cheaper electricity bill. Any utility trying to copy this model would gut its own revenue — that is the moat.

The underlying grid problem is **underutilisation**, not broken infrastructure. Poles and wires move energy through space; batteries move it through time. The grid is built for peak demand and runs wastefully the rest of the time. Distributed home batteries fix that without new transmission lines, and they sidestep the two structural limits of utility-scale storage: interconnection queues and transmission congestion.

Base scales two ways. In deregulated markets it goes direct to consumer. In regulated ones it sells megawatts-as-a-service to utilities. Electricity is a commodity — cheapest wins — so vertical integration is a cost weapon, not an ideology.

Internally, the company runs on written clarity. Zach keeps two colour-coded notebooks: red for working *in* the business today, black for working *on* it six to twelve months out. Monthly memos and nightly notebooks keep 500-plus people coherent and accountable. Physical anchors — the Turtle on the supply chain lead's desk, factory floor posters — hold the system when culture alone cannot.

The ambition is explicitly long-term. Zach frames Base as a fifty-year journey, and the filter for every hiring decision is simple: are you solving for the mission, or for a paycheck?

## The Grid's Real Problem

### "poles and wires move energy through space, and batteries move energy through time." [0:06:19]

The cleanest framing in the whole excerpt. One sentence that explains exactly why batteries are the unlock — not more generation, not more wires, just temporal arbitrage of existing supply.

### "we're not making more electricity, we're just using it more efficiently to drive the cost down." [0:07:30]

This deflates a common misconception fast. Base isn't a generation play — it's an optimisation play on infrastructure that's already massively overbuilt for peak demand.

### "over the last 20 years, electricity demand has grown at roughly a 2% compounded annual growth rate... we think it'll go to somewhere on the order of 10%, which is obviously a 5x" [0:03:40]

The demand thesis in a single number. A **5x growth rate** on a base as large as national energy is the kind of tailwind that makes the market-size question irrelevant — you just need to be well-positioned early.

## Base's Business Model Insight

### "I don't sell batteries, I sell electricity, and we're going to install this battery on your home." [0:15:34]

The counter-position thesis in one sentence. The business model is the moat — not the product. Incumbents can copy hardware; they can't easily copy a model that cannibalises their own revenue stream.

### "if I showed up and said I have the best home battery on the market... Your margin is my opportunity, right? Like these big guys are going to compete on price." [0:15:34]

Sharp competitive logic. Attacking on price alone is a dead end against incumbents with distribution. The only durable entry is a **structurally different model** they'd have to self-destruct to match.

### "it's because it's a way to deliver the best product to the customer and that's all that matters." [0:21:31]

Cuts through any cynicism about the ownership model. Retaining the asset isn't extractive — it's the **only way to actually arbitrage wholesale markets** on behalf of someone who has no trading desk.

## Distributed Beats Utility-Scale

### "It's only important if you're playing a cost focused game. Electricity is a commodity. The best form of commodity is the cheapest one. And if you're going to deliver that, you got to vertically integrate." [0:32:25]

Clean, unsentimental reasoning. Most founders talk themselves into vertical integration as a strategic flex — Zach frames it as a **logical constraint** of the commodity market. No hedging.

### "We could go buy lithium mine and start refining lithium... sure, you could, right?" [1:09:48]

Vertical integration has no natural ceiling — only a current scale ceiling. The discipline is knowing which layer to integrate *now* versus which to defer. Base is at final assembly; the roadmap goes much deeper over decades.

## Scaling the Two-Sided Market

### "What's your constraint? What's the constraint of the business?" [0:57:31]

The Elon-lineage discipline distilled to one question, asked on repeat. Most board conversations drift toward strategy and opportunity — this one never does. The constraint framing is a forcing function that makes everything else feel like noise.

### "It's kind of a cope, if you will, to talk about the other stuff." [0:59:00]

Talking about adjacent opportunities when you have an unresolved constraint is avoidance behaviour dressed up as strategy. Rare for a founder to name this honestly. Most pitch decks are elaborate cope.

### "This is a multi decade, 50 year journey that we're on." [1:10:44]

Year three thinking and year thirty thinking are categorically different. Founders who conflate them misallocate attention badly. The explicit time-horizon framing here is the useful tool — not the ambition itself.

## Notebooks and Operational Cadence

### "The red notebook is in the business, and the black notebook is on the business." [0:40:48]

Two physical objects enforcing a **cognitive mode switch**. The colour difference is trivially simple and that's exactly why it works — a hard context boundary you can hold in your hand. Worth stealing.

### "Working in the business is I'm blocking and tackling and solving problems that are blocking us today. Working on the business is thinking six months out, twelve months out, and kind of strategic war gaming and chess playing." [0:41:02]

This is the **clearest articulation** of the in/on distinction I've seen. Most founders conflate the two and wonder why they never get ahead of their problems.

### "If you measure something that's not useful, you're going to end up going off in directions that are not actually moving you forward." [0:43:34]

Metrics discipline — the **dashboards must earn their place**. The warning isn't just about having metrics; it's that bad metrics actively mislead. Leaders are expected to challenge whether the dashboard itself is signalling the right thing.

## Physical Anchors and Focus

### "There's a Turtle on the desk of the head of the supply chain and there's a team of people that are all working on solving that problem. There's a meeting every single day and there's an email that goes out every week to the entire company." [0:43:34]

The Turtle is a **constraint-visibility system** with teeth. It names the bottleneck publicly, assigns a squad, and runs a daily cadence until resolved. The physical object stops the business from quietly deprioritising a blocker.

### "Are you solving for the mission? Are you solving for a paycheck?" [1:12:03]

Mission orientation is the only thing that makes a permanent company coherent. The "start, scale, sell" industrial complex Senra mentions is the investor's game, not the founder's. Zach's framing — where the mission literally cannot be completed — is the structural reason to never sell.
