# Notes: Lessons From Backing The Best Founders In Fintech | Micky Malka

## Summary

Micky Malka's central claim is that **founder-led fintechs** — Stripe, Nubank, Revolut — will dominate the coming age of agentic AI, precisely because slow-moving banks cannot use new technology as a catch-up tool.

Malka runs Ribbit Capital as the first structure he has deliberately designed to last forever. He counts five previous exits as failures, because none were built for permanence. It took him twenty years of accumulated lessons to earn the right to build something indefinitely.

The firm's core process is writing. Ribbit spends twelve to eighteen months drafting a thesis essay before deploying any capital. Writing forces the kind of conviction that keeps you steady when markets swing against you.

His filter for founders is what he calls the **Isle of the Tiger**: a scientist's curiosity, a missionary's conviction, a partner's heart, an athlete's ambition, and an owner's obsession. The tell is authenticity — if the mission written on day one is still present years later, that founder wins.

Compounding runs through everything Malka believes. Relationships, ethics, and reputation compound across decades just as capital does. A lesson learned running Lemon Bank in Brazil in 2003 paid off directly in a Walmart-scale deal twenty years later. Reputation, he argues, is the only non-renewable resource — damaged once, it closes doors you never even see shut.

He closes with a message about time. Young people hold the most powerful asset of all: decades left to compound. His rule is simple — you only need to get rich once.

## Labels Block Clear Thinking

### "the more you walk through life allowing things to have label, the more difficult life is because then you cannot see through it." [0:01:29]

Labels force you to see only what you've been told to see. The constraint isn't social — it's cognitive. Categories are a tax on pattern recognition.

## Writing Forces Conviction

### "if you cannot have conviction, then you're going to be going in mood swings with the markets up and down." [0:04:26]

He ties writing directly to conviction, and conviction to playing the infinite game. Most people skip the writing and wonder why they fold at the first drawdown.

### "you will lose a little bit of the nuance because part of it, if you read them carefully enough... the pattern repeats and what you learn from it is not the decisions he makes, it's how in any environment he repeats the same pattern of decision making process." [0:07:25]

He's sceptical AI can surface this — the meta-lesson from Buffett's letters is the *consistency of process*, not the individual calls. Solid pushback on the "AI will summarise everything" instinct.

## Mobile to AI: Banker in Your Pocket

### "mobile was the way to put a bank in your pocket. Now AI is going to be the way to get you the banker in your pocket." [0:15:35]

David Velez of Nubank's framing. Clean compression of the shift from access to **agency** — the product stops being a tool and starts being a delegate.

### "I'd rather be behind. I'm a much better person being behind or ahead." [0:19:01]

Being ahead triggers his alarm — if things are working, the rules are about to change and you need to fall behind again to **innovate**. Contrarian but consistent with how most serious builders actually behave.

### "The best expression of humans are in the intersection between chaos and order. If you're too orderly, you don't innovate. If you're too chaotic, you don't communicate and you break." [0:24:39]

Dee Hock's "chaordic" principle — the optimal zone is deliberately uncomfortable. Worth mapping to how Loom itself is structured as it scales.

## The Isle of the Tiger Framework

### "the energy of a scientist, the conviction of a missionary, the heart of a partner, the dreams of an athlete, the obsession of an owner" [0:44:12]

The "Isle of the Tiger" framework in one sentence. This is rare — a founder-evaluation rubric that actually fits on a napkin and has real signal. The athlete/owner pairing is the bit most people skip; that's where the fakes get filtered out.

### "if we get that phone call, it means that we did 100 things right to deserve the right to be there, to help this person think through it" [0:41:43]

The measure of a real investor relationship: earning the 3am call, then not answering it — just helping them answer it themselves. That distinction between answering and helping-to-answer is the whole game. Most advisors can't resist inserting the answer; that's what breaks trust.

## Compounding Beyond Capital

### "it's compounding relationships and ethics and work and principles and love and passion and time and just don't care about the winning or losing" [0:55:40]

Malka's version of the Munger latticework: compound everything, not just capital. This sounds soft until you clock that the Walmart deal literally traces back to a near-bankrupt Brazilian bank in 2003. The compounding is real and verifiable, not a platitude.

### "you can tie every single one of our decisions that we've made were big decisions like that in the rivet life to something that happened to me 20 years before" [0:54:47]

The infinite game made concrete — not a philosophy, but a traceable decision tree across decades. Worth sitting with this if you're building anything with a long runway. Your current failures are inputs, not write-offs.

## Reputation as Non-Renewable Resource

### "It is the most valuable asset. It's the only Asset we have, it's a reputation. Everything else can come and go" [0:59:19]

Malka's Buffett-influenced position: reputation is the only thing that sticks, even after everything else is gone. The Palmer Luckey point Senra adds sharpens this — reputation damage is **invisible** in its consequences, which makes it more dangerous than any visible financial loss.

### "if they are true to themselves. The mission that they wrote on the first day is still present today. And when you find that authenticity, they win" [0:42:55]

The filter that separates long-term bets from short-term ones: does the founder's original mission still match their behaviour a decade in? "Faked it to make it" can produce short-term success — Malka concedes that — but he stops spending time with those people. The selection criterion is **consistency over time**, not performance at a point in time.

### "The best entrepreneurs never have to sell their company." [1:13:00]

The cleanest definition of the infinite game I've heard. Selling = admitting you couldn't build the vehicle. Ribbit is Malka's attempt to finally build the thing he never has to exit.

## Building Ribbit to Last Forever

### "I had to learn for 20 years how to get to the point to design the structure, the culture, the team, the knowledge, the aperture, the brand compound all those values to a place where we can do something for forever." [1:13:08]

Twenty years of apprenticeship before the real company. Most people would have called the earlier exits the win. He reframed them as tuition.

### "The rebel mindset is one where you know authentically and genuinely that something can be better and you're willing to give your life for it." [1:08:35]

Not rebellion as contrarianism — rebellion as **conviction**. The distinction matters: one is reactive, the other is directional.

### "You and I know a lot of people that get to a point in life when they stop to ask questions and they start to preach — that's past the rebel time." [1:08:35]

The breaking point he's trying to push to the last day of his life. This is the actual risk for anyone who gets successful early — **preaching replaces curiosity**.

## The Power of Time

### "You have the power of time. All of us here are in our last chapter. You're just starting your time. You will compound for the next 70 years of your life. So never forget the rule I'm about to tell you. You only need to get rich once." [1:15:53]

Munger telling a 24-year-old he's the wealthiest person in a room full of billionaires. The point isn't money — it's that **compounding time** dwarfs any current balance sheet.
