# Notes: Micky Malka, Founder of Ribbit Capital

## Summary

**Micky Malka** built Ribbit Capital on a single, simple refusal: never accept a label.

His argument is that labels are perceptual traps. The moment you accept a category, you see only what the category allows. Refusing them is not contrarianism for its own sake — it is the precondition for seeing what everyone else misses. That same logic runs through how he writes, how he hires, and how he holds investments.

Writing is his core discipline. He writes 12-18 month theses not to communicate ideas but to discover whether the ideas are real. If you can't compress the argument onto a napkin, the conviction isn't there yet. Conviction matters because without it, you move with market sentiment instead of against it.

His framework for founders — the **"Isle of the Tiger"** — took a decade to build: the energy of a scientist, the conviction of a missionary, the heart of a partner, the dreams of an athlete, the obsession of an owner. It is the lens through which every Ribbit investment passes.

The Lemon Bank story is the proof of the long-term model. In 2003, Malka sold a dot-com, moved to Brazil, and started a branchless bank. That bet compounded for nearly two decades into a Walmart fintech joint venture. He traces almost every major Ribbit decision back to something that happened twenty years before it.

His closing argument is about time. Reputation is the only asset that cannot be replaced — damage is invisible, as doors simply never open. And time, he tells younger listeners, is the one thing the already-wealthy cannot buy back. Compound it. You only need to get rich once.

## Why Labels Are Perceptual Traps

### "the more you walk through life allowing things to have label, the more difficult life is because then you cannot see through it. You just see what you've been told to see." [0:00:25]

Labels are an epistemic trap, not just a social one. Malka frames this as literally breaking your own perception — you stop seeing reality and start seeing the category. Relevant to any domain where received wisdom calcifies early.

## Writing as Conviction-Testing

### "Writing is everything. Because we can sit here and talk about ideas for hours, but if you're not willing to put them in writing, you will never learn what truly they are, how deep they are." [0:03:20]

Writing forces the gap between a vague idea and a real one to become visible. This is the same reason Bezos banned slides. Malka takes it further — essays that start at 20 pages must compress to a napkin, and if they can't, the thinking isn't done.

### "If you cannot have conviction, then you're going to be going in mood swings with the markets up and down." [0:04:26]

Conviction is the mechanism that lets you stay in the game when signals go negative. Without it you're just reactive. The writing process IS the conviction-building process — that's the structural point here, not just a productivity tip.

## Mobile, AI, and the Banker in Your Pocket

### "mobile was the way to put a bank in your pocket. Now AI is going to be the way to get you the banker in your pocket." [0:15:28]

Nubank's David Velez framing — clean, memorable, and directionally correct. The distinction matters: a bank in your pocket is a tool; a banker is a judgment layer. That's a qualitatively different product, not an incremental one.

### "AI has just become a rocket nitro rocket for this breed of technology companies to just accelerate even faster and separate themselves again." [0:17:14]

The incumbents were closing the gap in 2020-2022, but AI reset the race. Malka's point is that the infinite game periodically reshuffles — and the founder-led players are the ones who move when rules change.

## The Rebel Mindset

### "I'd rather be behind. I'm a much better person being behind or ahead. When I'm ahead... immediately my spider senses get all activated." [0:19:01]

Being ahead triggers complacency detection — he treats success as a warning signal, not a resting point. Counter-intuitive but consistent with how most great builders operate. Comfort is the actual risk.

### "The rebel mindset is one where you know authentically and genuinely that something can be better and you're willing to give your life for it." [1:07:42]

The clearest definition Micky gives of "rebel" — and
it's not contrarianism. It's **conviction + sacrifice**,
applied consistently.

Worth noting: this maps directly to how good founders
think, not how most VCs think.

### "You and I know a lot of people that get to a point in life when they stop to ask questions and they start to preach — that's past the rebel time." [1:08:43]

The **inflection point** he's describing is real and
observable. Most people hit it mid-career and don't
notice.

The goal of staying in question-mode indefinitely is
harder than it sounds — especially when you're winning.

## The Isle of the Tiger Framework

### "the energy of a scientist, the conviction of a missionary, the heart of a partner, the dreams of an athlete, the obsession of an owner" [0:44:12]

This is Malka's "Isle of the Tiger" — his five-trait filter for founder quality. Took them a decade to crystallise. Worth stress-testing your own patterns against this list — it's one of the cleaner frameworks I've seen that doesn't collapse into "passion and grit."

### "if we get that phone call, it means that we did 100 things right to deserve the right to be there, to help this person think through it" [0:41:43]

Malka's benchmark for relationship quality: earning the 3am call. And the discipline once you're on it — never answer the question, just help them answer it. That second part is where most advisors fail. The value isn't the answer; it's the trusted sounding board.

## Twenty Years of Compounding Decisions

### "you can tie every single one of our decisions that we've made were big decisions like that in the rivet life to something that happened to me 20 years before" [0:54:47]

Malka frames Ribbit's big moves as expressions of the infinite game — every major decision traceable to an experience 20 years prior. This is the compounding of *pattern recognition*, not just capital. Most people underestimate how long that takes to load.

### "it's compounding relationships and ethics and work and principles and love and passion and time and just don't care about the winning or losing" [0:55:40]

Malka's definition of compounding goes well beyond finance — relationships, ethics, consistency — the prescription is just to keep showing up. Sounds soft until you realise he parlayed a 2003 Brazilian branchless bank into a Walmart JV 17 years later. That's the proof of concept.

### "when we had sold our first .com company and then we used all the proceeds to start this bank in Brazil, I moved with my wife to Brazil" [0:52:36]

At 25, Malka and partner Wences Casares put every chip back on the table — no outside investors — and nearly lost it all when Brazilian macro wouldn't cooperate. The macro lesson is real: execution can be spotless and the environment can still kill you. That's not a reason to avoid risk; it's a reason to understand the table you're sitting at.

### "I had to learn for 20 years how to get to the point to design the structure, the culture, the team, the knowledge, the aperture, the brand compound all those values to a place where we can do something for forever." [1:13:08]

Twenty years of iteration before getting the vehicle
right. The **compounding** here isn't financial —
it's structural and cultural.

Ribbit as the sixth company, not the first, matters.
Most people don't get that many shots.

## Reputation as the Only Non-Fungible Asset

### "It is the most valuable asset. It's the only Asset we have, it's a reputation. Everything else can come and go" [0:59:19]

Malka's position: reputation is singular — not one asset among many, but the only one that sticks permanently. The Palmer Luckey angle Senra adds is practically useful: reputation damage is *invisible* — the meeting that doesn't happen, the door that silently closes.

### "The best entrepreneurs never have to sell their company." [1:13:00]

Strong framing — forces you to ask whether you're
building something permanent or just something
sellable.

## Key Arguments

1. **Labels destroy perception** — accepting a
   category means seeing through its filter,
   not through reality; refusing them is a
   precondition for spotting what others miss.

2. **Writing builds real conviction** — long-form
   thesis work (not decks, not talk) stress-tests
   ideas; the napkin compression test proves
   whether the thinking is genuine.

3. **Founder quality has a fingerprint** —
   Malka's "Isle of the Tiger" (scientist,
   missionary, partner, athlete, owner) is the
   filter Ribbit uses; it took a decade to develop.

4. **Long-termism is a compounding strategy**,
   not a value — the Lemon Bank → OnePay arc
   (2003–2020) shows the infinite game is
   literal, not metaphorical.

5. **Reputation is the only non-fungible asset**
   — everything else fluctuates; reputation
   compounds silently in both directions, and
   damage is invisible (doors never opened).

6. **Money is the lagging AI frontier** —
   blockchain rails + AI agents is where the
   next leap happens; founder-led fintechs
   are best placed to capture it.

7. **The rebel mindset is permanent questioning**
   — the moment you start preaching instead
   of asking, you're finished; time (Munger's
   point) is the only asset young rebels
   have that the wealthy don't.
