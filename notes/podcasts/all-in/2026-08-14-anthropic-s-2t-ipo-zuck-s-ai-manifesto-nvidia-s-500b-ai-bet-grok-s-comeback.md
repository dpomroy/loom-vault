# Notes: Anthropic's $2T IPO, Zuck's AI Manifesto, Nvidia's $500B AI Bet, Grok's Comeback

## Summary

Anthropic is on track for a **$2 trillion IPO**, built on three consecutive years of 10x revenue growth — a ramp with no historical precedent.

The panel notes the paradox clearly. Anthropic is losing marginal share to OpenAI, open-source, and Grok, yet is still growing at extraordinary speed. The IPO number is likely a banker leak; the expectation is it prices lower and trades higher.

The episode's central argument is that **centralisation** is the real AI danger — not individual misuse. Zuckerberg's open-source manifesto and the panel's investment thesis land in the same place: a world with diverse, competing models is structurally safer than one dominated by two or three government-backed giants. Banning open source, several speakers argue, hands geopolitical advantage directly to China.

This makes Anthropic's own regulatory push self-defeating. The six-month frontier lead over open models is the only thing that justifies premium token pricing. FDA or FAA-style approval processes would erase that lead faster than any competitor could.

On the infrastructure side, supply — not demand — is the binding constraint. Energy, data centres, and permitting are what limit revenue growth, not appetite for the product. Nvidia is responding by securitising GPUs on an aircraft-leasing model, lowering the cost of capital and unlocking finance-constrained demand. One panellist calls Nvidia the "Federal Reserve of AI." Grok gets a late mention as a largely overlooked model that is Pareto-dominant on several benchmarks — a signal the market narrative may be missing something important.

## Anthropic's Unprecedented Growth

### "on the margin, Anthropic is losing share to OpenAI, to Open Source and to Grok, and they're still growing so fast" [0:05:09]

The pie is growing fast enough to mask share loss — that's the key insight here. When a market is expanding this violently, share percentages are a lagging and misleading signal. Watch absolute revenue, not relative position.

### "Dario has said that Anthropic might be the only private company in the world at some point" [0:12:40]

In this maximalist vision — Anthropic, then governments, then nothing else. Gavin's immediate instinct is to take the under. Sacks calls it SBF-adjacent hubris. Both are right; this kind of talk is a red flag from a founder pre-IPO.

### "they've grown 10x year over year for the last 3 years. So it's growing exponentially. If that rate of growth were to continue, they'd hit a trillion dollars of ARR by the end of next year" [0:09:24]

The question isn't demand — it's physical supply: compute, energy, turbines. Extrapolating exponentials is always dangerous, but the constraint here is atoms not bits, which is a different and slower problem to solve.

## The Case for Open-Source AI

### "what's great about open source is it means that we're going to have a rich variety of AIs, we're going to have diversity of AIs. And that is a good world for humans" [0:25:41]

Gavin frames model diversity as the safety mechanism — not regulation, not alignment research. This is the cleanest articulation of the decentralisation argument in the excerpt. It also explains why his Grok investment is a principled bet, not just a financial one.

### "the notion that AI is so dangerous, that the only safe path is an extreme concentration of power seems inherently problematic" [0:28:38]

Zuckerberg's manifesto targets the core contradiction in EA-aligned AI safety thinking: absolute power granted to the enlightened few has a bad historical track record. Sacks links it directly to Sowell's *Vision of the Anointed* — intellectuals engineering society for its own good. That framing is more damning than anything Zuck wrote himself.

### "they should essentially work together. It should be made into a cartel" [0:31:30]

Sacks describes the actual Biden-era policy vision: two or three companies, an Atomic Energy Commission-style oversight body, and a merger of corporate and state power. That's not a conspiracy theory — it's the explicit frame he heard in rooms. Worth keeping as a reference point when reading future AI regulation proposals.

## Centralisation as the Real Risk

### "this technology is too dangerous to centralize" [0:33:40]

The cleanest framing of the whole open-source debate — and it lands because it mirrors the EA's own logic back at them. The decentralisation side wins this rhetorical round by accepting the danger premise and flipping the conclusion.

### "I do not want Dario Amadei or Anthropic deciding what's in my best interests. I just fundamentally don't want that." [0:34:33]

Raw statement of the individual-sovereignty case. Worth noting this isn't an argument against safety — it's an argument about *who decides*, which is the more durable objection.

### "if we ban open source, if we let this control be centralized, we will lose AI, we will lose geopolitics, we will lose everything to China. It's just that simple." [0:39:12]

The patriot framing is blunt but the underlying logic holds — you can't enforce global compute governance when China isn't party to it. The panel makes this explicit: the US has no jurisdiction over China, and the EA dream of global AI treaties was always a pipe dream.

## Nvidia Becomes AI's Central Bank

### "Nvidia is kind of becoming the central bank of AI, the Federal Reserve of AI." [1:06:39]

The analogy works: Nvidia sets the reserve rate (the residual value guarantee), private banks underwrite the deals, and liquidity flows downstream to compute buyers. Whether it's circular depends entirely on whether real cash flows back it — which, unlike 2008 mortgages, compute-rental revenue arguably does.

### "the numbers are getting so big that the TAM is getting constrained by the ability to finance this build out. And what he's doing is alleviating that finance constraint so that he can grow as big as the TAM actually is." [1:05:19]

This is the cleanest articulation of *why* Jensen did the deal — it's not a financial engineering trick, it's a TAM unlock. If the bottleneck is capital rather than demand or chips, removing it directly expands Nvidia's addressable revenue.
