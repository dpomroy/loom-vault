# Notes: Eric Vishria - A Decade of Lessons Investing in Software & Hardware - [Invest Like the Best, EP.486]

## Summary

Eric Vishria's central claim is that **inherited playbooks destroy value** when the underlying dynamics have already shifted.

On AI infrastructure, his sharpest finding is that running models well is a deep technical skill, not a commodity. Fireworks achieves 5x better performance than generic cloud providers on identical hardware — meaning inference expertise is a genuine moat. Enterprise AI adoption is moving faster than cloud ever did, because firms treat it as both existential threat and opportunity.

The old **SaaS moats** are dissolving. AI agents make database migration trivially cheap, so switching costs — the foundation of traditional SaaS defensibility — no longer hold. Winners will be defined by cost, elasticity, and iteration speed instead.

Go-to-market models are equally broken. Most AI-era companies were unknowingly built around push-demand sales frameworks, which actively misidentify where growth is bottlenecked. Pull-demand products require an entirely different measurement model.

Hardware investing follows a generational pattern — each compute shift mints roughly one $100B winner — but execution is brutally harder than software. A logical block diagram gets you 80% of the way in software; in hardware it gets you 2%.

On board partnership, Vishria's rule is chemistry-first: he only invests if he could honestly recruit someone he cares about to treat the company as their life's work. Growth-stage deals can now earn venture-style multiples, widening the opportunity set. On luck, he argues it is engineerable — frequency rises with surface area, through exceptional people and many high-quality bets. And on AI capability predictions, he notes that even Geoff Hinton was wrong: deployment friction, data gaps, and Jevons effects consistently confound confident technical forecasts.

## Inference Is Not a Commodity

### "The performance difference for a fireworks versus a cloud provider is like 5x and that is just the speed performance." [0:03:36]

Same open-source model, same Nvidia hardware — 5x speed gap, plus a separate throughput advantage that's invisible unless you know the unit economics. The commodity framing of inference is wrong. Deep systems expertise is the moat, not the model weights.

### "You actually really need to understand the nuances of model capabilities, what they're great at and where they fail." [0:14:29]

And pair that with deep customer-problem understanding — bridging the two is where valuable solutions come from. The three traits Eric names — taste, customer empathy, jagged-edge awareness — replace the old PM/designer/engineer triad. Job titles don't matter; the three things do.

### "It feels to me like we're going to end up with an oligopoly of winners." [0:08:50]

And below the oligopoly, genuinely crazy hundred-billion-dollar smaller winners — same structural outcome as cloud. The zero-sum framing ("Anthropic eats everything") is the same mistake investors made about AWS in 2007. History rhymes hard here.

## Enterprise AI Adoption Dynamics

### "We used to be building castles, now we're building sandcastles that are washed away." [0:13:21]

Brett Taylor's framing for how Sierra operates — obsoleting their own work every six months as model capabilities shift. This is the right mental model for Loom too. Anything that assumes a stable capability baseline will age badly inside a year.

### "The criteria changed. So now you're going to have a ton more applications that start." [0:19:32]

AI makes experimentation cheap — which means databases need to scale from near-zero to massive, not just handle a pre-specced enterprise workload. The stickiness that made legacy database companies great (migration pain, interface lock-in) is being dissolved by agents that don't mind doing the migration. The moat shifted, not the market.

### "Most companies in each of those areas are not going to work. And it's actually more important than ever to have real differentiation." [1:02:06]

Even in a rising tide, most boats sink. This is the correct pushback on the "AI lifts all boats" narrative. **Differentiation isn't optional** — it's load-bearing in a crowded infra stack.

## SaaS Moats Dissolving

### "Every single day that you are hitting your plan, you are destroying equity value." [0:21:19]

Everything we were taught said: set the plan, execute relentlessly, compound value. That logic has inverted for legacy SaaS companies that haven't moved to AI. Brutal but correct. Execution against the wrong hill is still the wrong hill.

### "fundamentally, without realizing it, everybody was implementing something that was based on pushing demand, not pulling demand." [0:25:27]

The old enterprise sales model is built backwards for this moment. If your product is genuinely magical, the quota capacity model doesn't just underperform — it's the wrong unit of analysis entirely. The insight generalises: any inherited framework built for friction-heavy selling breaks when demand is **latent and massive**.

## Founder-Led Go-to-Market

### "the best salesperson in any of these companies is the founder. And what they're doing is bridging the jagged edge to what the customer's capability is." [0:26:46]

Simple-sounding, but the implication is that the skill being rewarded here isn't sales craft — it's product depth and empathy combined. Hiring a traditional sales exec to replicate this usually just imports the wrong mental model.

## Hardware's Brutal Execution Gap

### "In software, if you have that logical block diagram of like why it works and everything else, you're kind of 80% of the way there. In hardware, you're like 2% of the way there." [0:32:14]

This is the sharpest single framing for why hardware investing is categorically different — not just harder in degree, but harder in **kind**. Software conviction converts to execution leverage; hardware conviction converts to a long list of unsolved physics problems.

## Board Chemistry and Investment Standards

### "if it doesn't work, it'll be for all of the reasons that your partner said. If it does work, it will be because those reasons didn't matter." [0:39:01]

One of the cleaner articulations of why base-rate thinking fails at the frontier. The skeptic is usually right — except precisely when it matters most that they aren't. Useful pushback to keep in mind when conventional wisdom blocks a genuinely novel bet.

### "Could I talk one of them into going to this company and, honestly, intellectually honestly to myself, explain to them why this could be their life's work? And if I can't do that, I should not invest." [0:50:00]

This is a **conviction test**, not a valuation test. It forces you to hold the opportunity to a human standard rather than a financial one — and is harder to fake to yourself. Worth stealing as a filter for any high-stakes commitment, not just venture.

### "If this person calls me at 9pm on a Saturday night, will I pick up the phone." [0:50:35]

Short and unambiguous. Partnership is a **bilateral relationship** — the green-button test cuts both ways and requires the founder to feel the same. Most investors skip this check and wonder later why the board dynamic is hollow.

## Growth-Stage Earning VC Returns

### "For most of the industry's history, two things were synonymous. Early stage investing and high cash on cash multiples." [0:52:23]

The quote frames the premise cleanly. The interesting move is what comes next — that the Venn diagram is *separating*. Worth tracking as a thesis for how Loom or any capital-light software product thinks about when to raise and at what stage.

### "The circle of high cash on cash multiple opportunities is bigger than just early stage." [0:52:43]

The claim is that larger outcomes have expanded where multiples are achievable — not that there are more opportunities overall. The nuance matters. This isn't a bull case for late-stage spraying; it's a narrow claim about a handful of exceptional companies.

## Luck as Engineerable Surface Area

### "Getting the ball close to the pin, that's hard work. Getting the hole in one, that's luck." [0:56:47]

The golf analogy: practice gets the ball close, but the hole-in-one is luck. The useful reframe is that **luck surface area is a function of volume and quality** of bets — not just randomness. Applies directly to how to think about positioning Loom and the racing tool: do the reps, let the outcomes surprise you.

## Capability vs Deployment Realities

### "Geoff Hinton's three orders of magnitude smarter than I am could not have been more wrong." [1:02:59]

Hinton called for stopping radiologist training in 2016; the underlying technical reasoning was correct, but the conclusion wasn't. The lesson: **technical correctness ≠ deployment correctness**. Smart people making deterministic predictions about AI disruption are making the same mistake — ignoring real-world friction.

### "In the ensuing time, we need more radiologists, not less." [1:04:47]

The transition is slow, and during it demand for radiologists *increases* because cheaper imaging means more imaging — Jevons Paradox. This is the best single argument against AI unemployment panic in this excerpt. The mechanism is concrete and generalisable: **lower cost → higher volume → more human labour needed in transition**, not less.
