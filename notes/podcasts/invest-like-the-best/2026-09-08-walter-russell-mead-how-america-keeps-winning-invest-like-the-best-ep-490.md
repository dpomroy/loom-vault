# Notes: Walter Russell Mead - How America Keeps Winning - [Invest Like the Best, EP.490]

## Summary

America is not declining — but the world order it built after 1945 is. Walter Russell Mead's central argument is that conflating **US power** with the post-WWII system is the single biggest error in foreign policy analysis.

To explain why America keeps surviving, Mead traces a 300-year pattern. From Louis XIV to Xi Jinping, every centralised land power has tried to break the English-speaking maritime order — and every one has lost. The hobbits, as he puts it, keep winning. That track record shapes how he reads the Trump moment: not as collapse, but as a coalition shift from 60 years of Hamilton-Wilson consensus toward a Jefferson-Jackson foreign policy.

Three structural dangers are converging at once. Tech firms, unlike old multinationals, need IP security and supply-chain sovereignty — so they are pulling toward state power rather than free markets. Drone warfare has rewritten frontline combat faster than doctrine can follow, and the US, as the biggest legacy-hardware power, is most exposed. Meanwhile, the three domestic classes that historically stabilise American foreign policy — labour, business, and technocrats — are all in disarray simultaneously.

The information revolution makes this harder, not easier. Unlike the Industrial Revolution, which made societies converge, it lets each country weaponise its own culture and language — fragmenting the shared ground that global order depends on.

Mead's one genuine source of optimism is **medical and AI velocity**. A drug that didn't exist a year ago is already extending lives in ways he has watched personally. If AI compresses healthcare costs at scale, the fiscal crisis may turn out to be far more manageable than the numbers currently suggest.

## Dynamic Country, Crumbling Order

### "It's this odd picture of a dynamic country in a collapsing world system." [0:02:56]

The sharpest line in the excerpt. Mead separates **American power** from the institutions that channel it — a distinction most commentary collapses. Worth holding: the system burning doesn't mean the country is losing.

## Trump as Spectacle and Coalition Shift

### "The Donald Trump show is the most compelling entertainment, infotainment spectacle that humanity has ever seen." [0:07:10]

Mead frames Trump not as a politician but as a **bandwidth monopolist** — no single living person has consumed as much of humanity's collective attention. Provocative framing, but it explains why normal reputational logic doesn't apply to him.

### "Trump has deployed that concept on an industrial scale." [0:07:06]

The argument: Clinton discovered shame only exists in *other people's* minds. Trump industrialised the insight — just keep moving, the caravan moves on. This is the **cleanest mechanical explanation** of Trump's political durability I've seen.

### "From the time of Louis XIV right up until now, you could sort of argue the dominant theme of Global power politics has been this rise of this English speaking, maritime, commercial liberal order." [0:10:09]

The same contest — open maritime power vs centralised continental power — has repeated through France, Germany, the Soviets, and now China. Useful **mental model**: every era has a new name on the continental side; the maritime side keeps the same playbook.

### "And yet somehow in the end the hobbits keep winning." [0:10:09]

Every time the centralised technocratic land power looked like it was walking away, the "ridiculous, confused" maritime alliance muddled through. The **hobbit framing** is doing real work — it captures that the winning side never *looks* like it should win. Worth keeping for Loom.

## Tech, Trade, and the New Hamiltonianism

### "If our AI companies, if our tech companies can only benefit from the internal American market, that's actually not enough to generate the revenue and growth that they need even to stay technologically equal, say, to China." [0:22:13]

Isolationism isn't just a foreign policy failure — it's a **commercial one**. Cutting off global markets kneecaps the tech lead America is currently banking on. Direct relevance: any trading tool or platform I build faces the same dynamic at smaller scale — TAM is everything.

### "Tech is actually the locus of international great power competition and China's attempt to build a technosphere that can dominate the American technosphere." [0:40:35]

To American tech companies this is critical to their business, and to the American state it's critical to power — much to the surprise of both worlds, they've been driven in on each other. The "I'm a global company" era is over. The re-nationalisation of tech is the structural shift that explains almost everything happening in Silicon Valley politics right now.

### "Your employees need to think it's an evil sin to sell IP to China. Your coworkers will rat you out in a heartbeat if they think you're doing something like that." [0:40:35]

Increasingly, companies are starting to realise they need their employees to be **patriotic** in this specific sense. Striking because it reframes corporate culture as a national security instrument. This isn't HR — it's counterintelligence by another name.

## The Information Revolution's Double Edge

### "A new technology just doesn't appear like an angel and grant wishes everywhere. You have to think really hard about how to make it work." [0:33:05]

Mead's framing here. Clean rebuttal to techno-utopianism. The AI hype cycle needs this injected into it repeatedly — the institutional and regulatory "infrastructure" is the hard part, not the model.

### "The Industrial Revolution forced societies to become similar to each other. The information revolution enables countries to use their own cultures, languages and so on as platforms." [0:48:17]

The industrial logic demanded institutional convergence toward a Western model; the information revolution reverses that pressure. The divergence thesis is underrated. Worth holding against the assumption that the internet = Westernisation — the MTV example that follows makes it concrete.

## Drone Warfare and Hardware Exposure

### "I talked to people who said that they don't treat bullet wounds anymore, it's all drones." [0:51:59]

The Ukraine war is no longer battalions shooting at battalions — drones have created a grey kill zone where at most you'd have a couple of soldiers together. One sentence that makes the battlefield shift visceral. The follow-on point about **63 days to evacuate one wounded soldier** is the detail that sticks.

## Domestic Classes in Disarray

### "All three of those classes are in total disarray. And yet we are facing a whole series of very serious changes, perplexities." [0:57:44]

The organised working class, the regulated business class, and the expert technocrat class — the three groups that balanced post-WWII America — have all collapsed as coherent forces, leaving the country structurally unequipped to answer what to do about AI or anything else. This is the most load-bearing claim in the excerpt. Not a political complaint — a **structural diagnosis**.

## Automating Deterrence and the Doomsday Problem

### "Automating your foreign policy decision making process increases deterrence." [1:11:20]

The logic is clean: credible deterrence requires certainty,
and humans introduce doubt. An AI that *will* respond
is more threatening than a human who *might*.

This is the dark flipside of every "AI alignment" conversation —
sometimes **unpredictability is the safety feature**,
and removing it has a strategic cost.

### "Are we really willing to have the Doomsday Machine decide whether we're going to go to war or use nuclear weapons or something like that." [1:11:20]

Mead lands on the core tension: full automation maximises
deterrence credibility but removes human override.

He asks it as a question, which is honest —
nobody has a clean answer. The **Doomsday Machine**
reference (Kubrick / RAND) is deliberate and apt.

## Medical and AI Velocity as Wildcard

### "A drug that didn't exist a year ago is keeping him not just breathing, but interacting with people and doing things." [1:13:30]

Mead uses Ben Sasse's terminal pancreatic cancer diagnosis
as a concrete case — not a cure, but a meaningful extension
of quality life from a drug that simply didn't exist yet.

Anecdote does more work than statistics here.
**Velocity of medical progress** is the real point —
the gap between "no treatment" and "treatment" is shrinking fast.

### "We're going to be seeing a cost revolution in healthcare that people will have 50 years from now better healthcare and cheaper healthcare than anybody has today." [1:13:30]

Mead extends this to fiscal policy: cheaper, better healthcare
means Medicare becomes less of a structural drag,
making entitlement deficits more tractable than they look now.

Bold claim. The **cost collapse** argument is the one
worth stress-testing — better outcomes and lower cost
simultaneously is not the historical pattern in healthcare.

## The Cornucopia We Can't Manage

### "Our biggest problem is that this cornucopia of wealth that the information revolution unlocks, the stuff is coming out of this cornucopia so fast, we just don't know how to manage it." [1:13:30]

Mead frames the bottleneck as absorptive capacity,
not innovation — the problem isn't generating breakthroughs,
it's institutions and society keeping pace with them.

This reframes most current political dysfunction as a
**transition problem**, not a terminal one. Plausible,
but it requires institutions to eventually catch up —
not guaranteed.

## Key Arguments

2. **Four permanent schools of US foreign policy**

   Trump is a Jefferson-Jackson coalition
   replacing 60 years of Hamilton-Wilson consensus.

3. **Tech forced into the state**
   Unlike legacy multinationals needing free trade,
   tech firms need **IP security and supply-chain
   sovereignty** — making them natural Hamiltonians
   aligned with government power.

4. **Drone warfare is a discontinuity**

   the US, as the biggest legacy-hardware power,
   is **most exposed** to that disruption.

5. **Three stabilising classes all in disarray**
   Labour, business class, technocrats —
   the domestic architecture needed to project
   global stability is missing exactly when
   **stakes are highest**.

6. **Medical/AI velocity is the real wildcard**
   Drug-development cycles compressing fast;
   if AI collapses healthcare costs, the
   **fiscal crisis may be self-correcting** —
