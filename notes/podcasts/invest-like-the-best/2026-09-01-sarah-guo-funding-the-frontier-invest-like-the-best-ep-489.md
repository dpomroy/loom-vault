# Notes: Sarah Guo - Funding the Frontier - [Invest Like the Best, EP.489]

## Summary

Sarah Guo's core bet is simple: **no single AI lab wins**, so back the best individual builders.

She opens with a provocative claim — recursive self-improvement in AI research models could produce exponential intelligence within two years. But the mood inside frontier labs is quietly fracturing. Many researchers now feel their individual contribution is irrelevant, either because models will do the work anyway, or because only compute scale matters. Guo thinks the talent implications of this are badly underreported.

The infrastructure holding AI back is not technical. Energy and data centre build-out is blocked by regulation and public consent, not engineering capability. She frames **compute independence** as the new energy independence — a national-security-level dependency that requires active investment across the full stack, not just chips.

On capital allocation, she is openly critical. Too much money is moving on founder pedigree alone, without any real opinion on whether the technical thesis holds. Her own process runs the opposite direction: she starts at conviction and works backwards, suspending doubt until the bet resolves.

Two specific reversals stand out. She used to doubt that AI could sell into pharma — actual contract data changed her mind. And on open source restriction, she argues the policy is self-defeating: it slows law-abiding American businesses while leaving adversarial actors completely unaffected.

Her closing frame is optimistic but precise. AI raises the ceiling on what productive people attempt — she works *more* since becoming more productive, not less. And Silicon Valley's durable edge, she argues, is a culture that evaluates ideas and people on their merits, not their credentials.

## The Exponential Intelligence Thesis

### "The belief is with recursive self improvement of AI research models that can improve the models themselves. We are a year, two years away from some sort of exponential intelligence." [0:11:33]

This belief is new within the last 12 months for a lot of researchers. Worth flagging: Karpathy has reportedly said "two years away" for a decade. Treat the sentiment as a signal of mood, not a prediction.

## Researcher Morale Fracturing Inside Labs

### "I definitely think there's a large contingent of researchers who would feel that one of two things is now true. What I do doesn't matter anyway because the model is going to do it. Or two, the only thing that matters is compute scale." [0:12:50]

Both of those are somewhat disempowering. This is the most interesting structural observation in the excerpt. If top researchers feel irrelevant to their own field, the talent dynamics at the frontier labs get weird fast.

## Infrastructure as the Real Bottleneck

### "I don't think we lack the technical and entrepreneurial capability to build abundant cheap energy for the data centers." [0:15:27]

The bottleneck isn't technology or capitalism — it's regulatory and political alignment: convincing people that data centers and nuclear belong in their backyard. Sober and correct. The constraint is **permitting**, not physics.

### "There is not a version of the world where we rebuild our industrial base without automation." [0:39:41]

The reindustrialisation argument collapses without AI and robotics — US labour costs make the maths impossible. Guo is blunt about this, which most politicians won't be.

## Pedigree-Proxied Investing

### "People are making large scale research bets without any intuition for them or without any opinion on them. And I think not having a point of view on the business besides the pedigree of the person is dangerous." [0:17:23]

Sarah's concern is that investors are proxying judgment to pedigree rather than developing genuine technical intuition about the bet itself. Direct relevance to how *any* early AI investment should be stress-tested — founder quality is necessary but not sufficient.

### "Other people, I think, are like, more even in their decision making. They're, like, thinking about it and thinking about it, and they climb to conviction versus I start there and then I, like, work backwards." [0:24:23]

The key similarity between Sarah and her partner Mike is they'll start at a point and explain what will move them. Good decision-making pattern to steal: **declare your prior, then specify what would change it** — rather than gathering data until something forces a conclusion.

### "The ability to take a point of view that is not obvious in the market... and then just suspend doubt and act with full belief until it's true or not. That's a great way to be a partner to somebody building a business." [0:48:41]

This is the actual definition of conviction investing, and it's rarer than it sounds. Most VCs hedge verbally while the founder needs someone fully in the tent.

## Shipping vs. Believing: Nothing Is True Until Shipped

### "Nothing is true until it is shipped. This entire team believes that we are going to have general semi humanoid robots doing things in people's homes. First and beta end of this year." [0:21:26]

That is not something that any of us believed. Extraordinary claim with a hard deadline — worth revisiting at year-end. **Sunday Robotics** is the name to watch.

## Open Source Restriction Is Self-Defeating

### "If you did restrict use of open source models in the United States, you'd basically just restrict law abiding American businesses and slow them down... because the actual attackers or people who have adversarial uses of these things are not affected by your restrictions." [0:37:27]

Classic security policy error — restrictions impose cost only on the compliant. The adversarial actors route around. Guo states this cleanly and it's the strongest argument against open source crackdowns.

## Compute and Biology: Two Thesis Flips

### "I want to spend my energy figuring out if we're 1% of the way in, what is the next 99% of diffusion?" [0:52:59]

Sharp reframe — most investing energy in AI goes to lab strategy debates, which Guo explicitly calls out as a waste. Diffusion curve thinking is the **actually useful** frame for application-layer bets.

### "In pharma the debate is does it change with models? I'm a strong yes." [0:46:38]

Guo moved from sceptic to conviction on AI-in-biology based on **empirical** portfolio data — Chai Discovery closing $10M contracts with top-10 pharma. Conclusion-first investing from real signal, not narrative.

## AI Raises the Ceiling, Not Lowers the Floor

### "I'm hopeful that a year from now we see Jevons paradox in practice. As we have agents and products that do more of the mundane more effectively in all the domains of our lives." [0:53:40]

Jevons paradox: cheaper resource → more consumption, not less. Guo expects AI efficiency gains to **expand** total demand for AI tasks rather than contract it. Software engineering is already showing this pattern in her portfolio.

### "Do you work less now that you are more productive with AI? Yeah, I work more." [0:54:48]

The honest answer nobody wants to say out loud. AI as a productivity tool expands appetite, not leisure — more gets done so more gets attempted.

The Jevons paradox applied to knowledge work. Worth keeping as a one-liner counter to the "AI will free up your time" pitch.

## Silicon Valley's Edge: Ideas Over Credentials

### "I think there are so many people who are extraordinarily accomplished in Silicon Valley who care very little for pedigree." [0:55:48]

The dominant factor in their willingness to invest is **assessment of the idea and the person** — not where you went or who you know.

This is the thing that makes SV structurally different — and also explains why it keeps pulling global talent away from credential-heavy ecosystems like finance or academia.

### "The dominant factor in their own willingness to invest in a relationship or a person is just their assessment of the idea and the person." [0:55:48]

Sarah flags this as **rare** — most ecosystems don't work this way.

Relevant as a counter to the "it's all who you know" cynicism. The network matters, but the filter is still the idea.

### "You can take any task in any job and just try to be great at a task with mimicry and first principles thinking." [0:56:48]

Her defence of hiring and being a young, inexperienced VC — you don't need domain authority to do the work well if you approach it with the right method.

Clean articulation of a generalist mindset. Mimicry to bootstrap pattern recognition, first principles to stress-test it. Useful framing for early-stage anything.

### "You don't need any particular advantage to have an idea." [0:57:59]

The point is that **ideas are democratically accessible** — what matters is whether someone will evaluate yours on its merits.

Slight caveat: access to the people who will evaluate it fairly is itself a structural advantage. The framing is optimistic but not quite complete.
